> **When honest observations, speech articulation, and humor are misread by others, and the exhausting reality of bridging the communication gap.**

A recurring, often painful theme throughout my life as an undiagnosed autistic individual was the stark disconnect between my internal intent and how my words or actions were perceived by authority figures, family, and peers. It is a fundamental truth of the neurodivergent experience: we are constantly speaking a different language than the neurotypical world around us, and the burden of translation almost always falls on our shoulders.

## Speech Articulation & Auditory Perception

I have a subtle speech lisp (similar to the cadence and articulation of artist George Watsky). Growing up, this led to frequent, baffling misunderstandings. Whenever I exclaimed *"Geez!"* out of frustration or surprise, my mother frequently misheard it as *"Jesus!"* and sternly reprimanded me for swearing. 

No matter how many times I earnestly clarified the exact word I had spoken, the auditory perception of the listener overrode my spoken reality. My truth was irrelevant because their perception dictated the consequence. This taught me early on that intent doesn't matter if the delivery doesn't match the neurotypical expectation.

Similarly, during a drive to a baseball game in Paradise, my parents commented that a specific traffic turn arrow *"doesn't stay green for very long."* Watching the light change to yellow in a fraction of a second, my brain processed their statement as a factual observation requiring confirmation. I agreed literally: *"Yeah, that light really doesn't stay on for long."* 

Both parents instantly interpreted my earnest, factual agreement as passive-aggressive, mocking sarcasm, resulting in an unexpected and highly stressful conflict in the car. The exhaustion of constantly defending my sincerity against accusations of sarcasm was immense.

## Social Timing & Accidental Offense

In Grade 12, while on a cadet trip held on Father's Day, I was in a jovial mood and went around jokingly wishing fellow cadets *"Happy Father's Day."* It was a simple, repetitive social script that I was enjoying. 

Continuing the joke as a new cadet sat down at the table, I delivered the line. But I immediately realized with absolute horror that his father had tragically passed away a month prior in a highly publicized parade accident. 

Filled with immediate, suffocating dread and experiencing an internal shutdown, I retreated silently into myself. I couldn't formulate the words to explain that it was an automated script, not a cruel jab. Thankfully, a volunteer officer who understood my character and processing style stepped in to de-escalate the situation, recognizing it was a tragic, innocent lapse in situational awareness rather than an act of malice.

## The Grade 3 Classroom

One of my most vivid, visceral memories of taking things entirely too literally happened in Grade 3. The teacher, needing to step out of the room for a minute, pointed at me and said, "You are in charge to make sure nobody talks."

To a neurotypical child, this is a rhetorical device meant to invoke peer pressure and keep the class quiet. To my neurodivergent brain, this was a sacred, literal directive from the ultimate authority figure. I was *in charge*. I had a *mission*.

The other kids immediately figured out my literal interpretation and started intentionally getting me going. They would whisper, talk, and giggle. In response, I would physically get out of my desk, walk over to confront them, and earnestly ask them to please stop talking, as per the teacher's instructions. 

When the teacher finally got back, she found me out of my seat, pacing around the classroom like a frantic hall monitor. She got really upset with me, reprimanding me for being out of my desk, and seemed genuinely taken aback. I guess she didn’t expect me to take her instructions so literally? I still don’t know. The cognitive dissonance of being punished for doing exactly what I was told to do was incredibly confusing and humiliating at the time.

## The Mr. Dressup Misunderstanding

Another vivid example of this literal translation failing me happened when I was 4 or 5 years old. My mom asked me if I wanted to "go see Mr. Dressup." 

For whatever reason, even though I had been to shows at a theater in the past (like seeing Sharon, Lois & Bram), my brain processed her question completely literally. I thought she meant we were going to literally drive to his actual house and go inside to visit him. As a highly shy child, the thought of walking into a stranger's house was terrifying, so I immediately said no. 

Later, when I realized she meant going to a theater to watch a show, I felt a deep, quiet sadness about having missed out entirely because of a simple misunderstanding. That's the invisible tax of literal processing: you miss the experience because you read the instructions too closely.

## Processing the Patterns

Understanding these patterns in adulthood has been life-changing. Recognizing that my brain operates on literal data processing—while neurotypical social communication relies heavily on invisible variables like tone, subtext, implied emotion, and unspoken context—has provided deep clarity. It doesn't erase the embarrassment of past misunderstandings, but it replaces the self-blame with profound self-compassion.
