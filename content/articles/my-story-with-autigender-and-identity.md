> **I'm not a stereotypical manly man. I cry. I get emotional. But that doesn't make me want to transition in any way.**

---

## What Autigender Means

Autigender is a concept I'm still learning about and uncovering. But in its simplest form: **your autism affects your gender in some capacity.**

Not that being autistic makes you trans. Not that it makes you nonbinary. Not that it makes you anything specific at all. It means that the cognitive and neurological experience of being autistic shapes how you understand, express, and relate to gender in ways that don't necessarily match the cultural scripts you were handed.

For most autistic people, the scripts were always confusing. Gendered social norms, the unspoken rules about how boys are supposed to act and what men are supposed to want — these were part of the larger social algorithm I was reverse-engineering my whole life without knowing it was an algorithm. Other people seemed to have absorbed the rules naturally. I had to construct them manually, observation by observation.

And somewhere in that process of construction, I kept running into the same uncomfortable truth: some of the rules didn't match what I actually felt or wanted.

---

## The Childhood Logic

Some early memories sit differently now than they did when I didn't have a framework.

I wanted an Easy-Bake Oven — not because I wanted to be a girl, but because I wanted to make myself cookies and cakes and eat them. The logic was completely straightforward to me. The gendering of the object was confusing and arbitrary. My father thought it would make me gay. I didn't get the oven.

In kindergarten, I genuinely enjoyed taking home the class teddy bear and taking care of it. Nurturing. Caregiving. These felt natural to me. They also weren't coded as things boys were supposed to enjoy.

Before puberty, I had thoughts I couldn't name at the time — genuine uncertainty about whether I had been made the right way, questions about what it meant to be a boy when so many of the signals associated with boyhood didn't feel like mine.

I don't tell these stories as evidence of anything in particular. I tell them because they're part of a pattern that made no sense until I had a better map of myself.

---

## Where I Actually Land

I'm not trying to stake a political claim here. I'm not interested in using my experience to make a larger argument about gender theory.

What I can say honestly is this:

I cry. I get emotional. I feel things intensely and express them in ways that don't fit the masculine performance most people around me were doing. I care deeply about nurturing, about relationships, about being emotionally present in ways that were often read as weakness or strangeness.

None of that means I want to transition. None of that means I don't identify as a man. I do. It just means that the *way* I inhabit that identity doesn't look like the template.

Autigender resonates because it gives a name to the experience of not quite fitting the gendered categories you were assigned, without requiring that you throw those categories away entirely. My autism shaped how I process and express gender the same way it shaped how I process and express everything else — through a different lens, with different rules, toward different conclusions than most people around me reached.

I'm still figuring out what that means in practice. But naming it has made the exploration feel less like something to hide and more like something worth understanding.

---

## The Disconnect Between Internal and External

There's a specific texture to being a man who cries at concerts, who wanted to bake as a child, who finds nurturing a baby profoundly meaningful, who has always felt slightly outside of the masculine social world — while still not experiencing any pull toward a different gender.

The mismatch isn't between me and my gender. It's between my gender and the cultural wrapper that was supposed to come with it.

Autism makes everything like this. The internal experience is genuine and clear. The gap between that experience and the external social expectation is where the friction lives. Learning that friction has a name — autigender — doesn't resolve it. But it makes it legible. And legibility is the beginning of everything.
