# Tone Policing and Ableism

I recently watched a [video clip](https://x.com/FoxNews/status/2095606784882860540) that perfectly encapsulates a subtle, yet deeply pervasive issue: the intersection of tone policing and ableism. 

In the video, a woman in pink is tone policing a woman in black. To me, it is clear that the woman in black is simply passionate about the subject. She seems to feel like she isn't being heard, and that lack of validation—combined with her passion for her opinions and stance—is naturally amplifying her demeanor. She is being vulnerable by going against the prevailing narrative.

Instead of engaging with the substance of her argument, the woman in pink repeatedly tells her to stop speaking in "that tone," implying that the woman in black is just getting unreasonably angry at her. 

Ironically, I suspect the woman in pink would be the first person in line at a disability rights parade. She might even identify as neurodivergent herself—who knows. But regardless of her background, what she is doing in this video is fundamentally ableist. 

As someone who has frequently been told to "calm down" and who often has trouble making their external tone match how they internally feel, my heart went out to the woman in black. 

Tone policing is something small that I don't think most people immediately recognize as ableist. But when you break it down—when you look at how it penalizes people for not communicating within a narrow, neurotypical, and "acceptable" emotional bandwidth—how isn't it? Demanding that someone modulate their passionate, frustrated, or dysregulated tone before you'll listen to their words is a mechanism of silencing. It disproportionately affects those who already struggle to be heard.
