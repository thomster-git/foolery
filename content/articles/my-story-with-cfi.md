In 2017, I got my first start at Canada Fluorspar Inc. (CFI) doing QA/QC for Civil Engineering. Before I could even walk onto the site, I managed to fail my pre-employment drug test for cannabis. I panicked. I had even bought a flush kit and spent days overhydrating, but it still caught me. I had to wait a long, stressful month before I could try again. Luckily, I passed the second time and finally started work.

When I arrived, my supervisor was pretty skeptical of me for the first couple of days. He had a mechanical engineering background, and we were doing civil work. But I'm a fast learner, and I tend to think from the bottom up. I didn't just want to know how to perform a test; I needed to understand *why* each test existed. Whether it was particle size analysis through wet and dry sieving, Proctor compaction tests, specific gravity tests, or water replacement tests, I dug in. Eventually, I proved my worth, and we got along well.

But that first stint ended on a weird note. Unbeknownst to me, I got blacklisted. I had occasionally returned to my residence to perform specific gravity tests—nobody knew why I was doing it, and instead of asking, people just talked shit behind my back. It was absurd, and I had no idea it had even happened until much later. 

By 2019, they needed people for the same work and brought me back in. First, though, I had to sit through a formal interview where they finally explained the whole blacklist situation and why they were willing to reconsider me. We cleared the air. I did QA/QC again for a bit, then transitioned to day shift project engineering. 

Project engineering was a chaotic environment. Our capital project team was incredibly under-resourced, too small for the scope of work, and drowning in constant ad-hoc requests. On top of that, management was constantly breathing down our necks, demanding miracles yesterday. A perfect example of the madness: we bought a massive storage tent to store concentrate. We checked the forecast and saw a severe wind storm coming, so the engineering team strongly advised waiting to set it up. Management said, "PROCEED." The tent didn't last 24 hours. It was ripped to shreds in the storm. We just had to stand there and watch.

Eventually, I moved to a Junior Mine Engineer role, which was a bit more grounded. I spent my days designing mine pits, calculating volumes, planning dumps and rehabilitation, and doing endless reporting. From there, I took my final role before everything fell apart: Mobile Maintenance Planner. 

Then came the receivership. I lost my job exactly five days before my son was born. The communication from the company during that period was abysmal. We were all left completely in the dark, waiting for answers that never came. A few weeks later, after my son's traumatic birth and being stuck in St. John's, the company casually asked me to come in and collect the stuff from my desk. There was no sympathy, no extended hand, no "Hey, we know you've got a lot going on, can we box this up for you?" Nothing. 

In 2024, CFI was under new ownership. I decided to go back. I had a house in the area, a young kid, another on the way, and I needed the money. It was the devil I knew. 

This time, I came back as an IT Technician, and within roughly six months, I was promoted to IT Coordinator. It felt like I was finally in the right place, combining my engineering background with a real passion for technology, systems, and problem-solving. But it wasn't exactly smooth sailing.

Actually recommending solutions that would work was always an uphill battle. My work was often pigeon-holed, forcing me to spend hours upon hours of research just to explain why a certain path was correct—only to be sent an alternative that completely missed the mark. We ran on a single domain controller on a dying server, and nobody above me would advocate for the solutions I was pushing. 

The infrastructure was equally neglected. Nothing in their network equipment was picked out with purpose. They went with standard office switches and the lowest tier firewall, completely ignoring the fact that we were in a harsh, dusty mining environment. There were no ruggedized components and no SOP for cleaning or managing the network equipment. Even worse, all the switches were sitting outside of their lock boxes for anyone on site to access. I tried pushing for basic IT policies for over six months, with absolutely zero movement from management. 

This was the job and the chaotic infrastructure I was sitting at when the Akira ransomware attack happened. But that’s a whole other story. 

Looking back, CFI has shaped my career in ways I never expected, for better and for worse. The management might have been absurd at times, and the ending of my first run was a disaster, but I wouldn't have found my way into tech—or learned exactly how *not* to design infrastructure—without it.
