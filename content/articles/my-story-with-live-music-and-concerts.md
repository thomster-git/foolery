My very first concert wasn't exactly a stadium tour. It was Tanzie and Roseabelle at a mall in Cambridge, Ontario. I was a huge fan of Treehouse—the kids' TV channel—at the time, but even then, I felt distinctly out of place being so excited about it. I was definitely the oldest kid there. I vividly remember looking over at my mom and thinking she was absolutely judging me for being so into it.

The first *real* concert came a few years later, in Grade 8. My aunt, who was pregnant at the time, took me to see Billy Talent in St. John's, with illScarlett opening. That night changed everything for me. Even now, I can still picture exactly where we were sitting. The noise, the raw energy, the feeling of the bass shaking the seats—it was a revelation.

Since then, live music has been a space where I go to feel something without having to explain it to anyone. The contrast between how I experience concerts and how some other people do is always interesting to me. A concert is supposed to be an immersive, full-body experience. You go to lose yourself in the music. But a lot of people just treat it as a loud social gathering, standing in a circle with a beer and barely watching the stage.

That's why when the Iceberg Alley Performance Tent rolled around in St. John's, I actively sought out the VIP section at the JAG Sky Bar for multiple Billy Talent shows. It captures the comfort—private bar, bathrooms, good food, a place to sit if you need it—without sacrificing the immersion. It's way more comfortable than being crushed on the floor. Of course, you can still dance and rock out up there, as long as you're ready to be judged by those people just standing in a circle drinking.

At my last Iceberg Alley concert, I was completely in it—dancing, jumping, singing along to every word. A friend of mine tapped me on the shoulder and pointed out that the people nearby were giving me the stink eye for rocking out. Imagine that: getting the stink eye for rocking out at a rock show.

There's a lot of emotion tied to these shows for me. Take Aaron Solowoniuk, Billy Talent's drummer. For the first two Iceberg Alley shows I went to, Aaron was able to come out and play a few songs. For the third show, he didn't play at all. I already knew about his battle with MS, but it still hit differently seeing a childhood hero struggling like that.

But live music isn't just about punk and nostalgia — it's about the pure kinetic energy of it. 

When the Skrillex/Jack Ü era was at its peak, I went to *Paint or Dye* for a DJ Bl3nd concert. That was such an incredibly fun show. I did so much dancing. My friend, who I also suspect is AuDHD, started moshing when the main act dropped "Let the Bodies Hit the Floor" into his set. He bumped into a girl who got *really* mad at him, giving him a two-handed shove to the chest. I don't even think he registered the push, but I quickly put my arm around him and moved him somewhere else.

Later that night, I was at a bar in the bathroom and someone literally told me that he wished his stuff (molly) was as good as mine. At this point, I wasn't even drunk. I had just danced for over an hour jumping up and down, and I was desperately trying to get as much water down as possible because I was cramping. I wasn't on anything — I was just completely buzzing from the life high, adrenaline, and pure autistic energy.

But live music brings intense joy, too. During one of the Billy Talent shows at Iceberg Alley, I was going through a period of autistic burnout. Right there in the middle of the set, I got completely overwhelmed with the moment. I started crying—not from sadness, but because I was genuinely happy and grateful. It was tears of living inside something beautiful. That's what live music is to me: an immersion that makes everything else disappear.
