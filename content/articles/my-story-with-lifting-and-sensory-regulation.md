> **Being teetering on the edge of sensory overload almost helps with heavy compound lifting. That realization changed how I think about my nervous system entirely.**

---

## The Environment Matters More Than You Think

There are certain variables in a training environment that most people treat as incidental. For me, they're the difference between a productive session and one that goes sideways before it starts.

**Temperature**: Training in a cool environment, or at minimum having fans running, isn't just a comfort preference. Heat is one of my fastest sensory escalators. When I'm already pushing toward physical capacity on a heavy squat or deadlift, ambient heat adds an additional layer of sensory noise that competes for neurological bandwidth I need for the lift itself.

**Glasses**: I don't wear my glasses during heavy compound lifts. They slide. The moment they shift on my face, my attention fractures — suddenly part of my brain is managing where my glasses are instead of bracing, breathing, and executing. Removing that variable removes that interruption.

**Sweat**: Same principle. A towel within reach for face-wiping isn't optional for me. Sweat on my face during a heavy set is a sensory intrusion I can't unhear once it starts. Controlling it proactively means it doesn't become a mid-set distraction.

None of these are unusual accommodations. They're just things I've learned about myself through paying attention.

---

## The Threshold Discovery: Overload as an Advantage

Here's the thing I didn't expect to learn about myself:

**Being on the edge of sensory overload actually helps with maximal effort compound lifting.**

I don't fully understand the neurological mechanism. But practically, what I've noticed is that when the nervous system is already highly activated — elevated heart rate, heightened sensory sensitivity, a kind of electric aliveness in the body — that activation state translates directly into force output on the bar.

The body is already primed. The inhibitory signals that usually govern how hard your muscles fire — the ones that exist to protect you from injuring yourself — seem to be partially suppressed when you're in that heightened state. Elite powerlifters call it "getting into the zone." For me, what gets me there has the texture of controlled sensory intensity.

---

## Provincials: The PR and the "Calm Down"

The clearest example of this happened at provincials.

I had just hit a **massive personal record** — a lift I'd been chasing. The adrenaline was coursing. I'd used smelling salts in the warmup room and that sharp ammonia hit had done exactly what it's supposed to: spike the nervous system into maximum activation.

When I came off the platform I was genuinely elated. I was talking loudly, gesturing, telling one of the guys from my gym — someone who competes at the same meets — how the lift went. I was excited in the way I get excited when something significant happens, which is: completely, physically, verbally.

He looked at me and said: *"Okay man, that's good, just calm down."*

I wasn't being disruptive. I wasn't out of control. I was celebrating a PR at a powerlifting meet. But my expression of excitement registered as too much — too loud, too intense, too uncontained — and the reflexive social response was to bring me down.

It's a small moment. But it's also a familiar one. The way I experience and express emotional highs has always read as disproportionate to people around me. What feels like appropriate enthusiasm from the inside looks like dysregulation from the outside. That gap — between internal state and external perception — is something I've been navigating my whole life without knowing why.

Knowing I'm autistic doesn't make that moment go away. But it does mean I can name it, and understand it, rather than just absorbing it as evidence that something is wrong with me.

---

## Training as Nervous System Regulation

The deeper truth about the gym for me isn't about aesthetics or competition. It's that **heavy compound training is one of the most reliable tools I have for nervous system regulation**.

When everything is overstimulating — noise, social demands, cognitive load, uncertainty — a barbell is one of the few things that demands total present-moment attention. You cannot be anywhere else during a heavy squat. The physics, the bracing, the breathing, the execution — it pulls all of the scattered neurological energy into a single, focused channel.

And the proprioceptive input of heavy loaded movement — the pressure of the bar on your back, the resistance through your entire chain — functions like a full-body deep pressure input. Similar to why weighted blankets help some autistic people. The nervous system responds to it differently than it does to ordinary exercise.

I'm still learning the language of my own body. But the gym has been part of that education in ways I didn't understand until recently.
