In the middle of a high-stakes emergency—like the fourteen days I spent rebuilding sixty computers and five servers from bare metal after the Akira ransomware attack—my brain doesn't panic. Everything narrows down to the task in front of me, and I can work eighteen hours a day on pure problem-solving momentum.

The bill always arrives the day after the crisis ends.

## When the Main Breaker Trips

People often confuse autistic burnout with ordinary tiredness or depression. Ordinary tiredness clears up after a good Saturday night of sleep. Autistic burnout feels like the main breaker in your nervous system has tripped: fluorescent lights hum too loudly, overlapping voices in a room become impossible to parse, and even simple decisions feel like wading through wet concrete.

## Building a Practical Regulation Kit

Learning how my nervous system actually works meant treating sensory load the same way an engineer treats thermal headroom—you don't wait for the chip to thermal-throttle before you add cooling:
* **Active Noise Cancellation:** Keeping my Bose QuietComfort headphones within arm's reach during deep focus blocks and noisy travel so background HVAC hum and cross-talk don't slowly drain the battery.
* **Proprioceptive & Sensory Outlets:** Having simple sensory tools at the desk instead of white-knuckling through eight hours of screen stress.
* **Protecting Recovery Windows:** Recognizing that a two-week emergency sprint borrows energy from next month, and building quiet, low-input recovery days into the schedule before the breaker trips on its own.
