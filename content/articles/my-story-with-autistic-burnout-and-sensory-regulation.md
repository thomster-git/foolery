## When the Medication Worked (And What It Revealed)

Starting ADHD medication was one of the most clarifying experiences of my life. The constant static — the executive dysfunction, the inability to start tasks, the paralysis of too many open loops — quieted down enough that I could actually function with intention for the first time.

But something unexpected happened.

With the ADHD noise reduced, the Autistic traits I had been unconsciously masking for three decades became impossible to ignore. The sensory sensitivities. The social energy depletion. The rigid routines that I'd always chalked up to "just how I am." The complete exhaustion after social interactions I'd powered through by sheer force of will.

I wasn't just ADHD. I was **AuDHD** — and the Autism had been there the whole time, hidden underneath years of compensatory masking and the relentless cognitive overhead of fitting in.

---

## What Autistic Burnout Actually Feels Like

Autistic Burnout isn't like work burnout or stress exhaustion. It doesn't resolve with a vacation or a good night's sleep.

It's a neurological depletion. The masking that holds your social performance together starts to fail. Things that were once manageable become genuinely painful. Routines that got disrupted feel catastrophic. Social interactions that you used to navigate on autopilot now require conscious energy you simply don't have.

For me it showed up as: complete emotional flatness, inability to tolerate noise or sensory input, an almost physical revulsion at unexpected changes, and an overwhelming need to be in my own controlled environment doing my own thing on my own timeline.

I didn't have a name for it for most of my life. That's the thing about being a high-masking autistic person with a high IQ — you figure out workarounds, you catalog social rules, you build a mental algorithm for "how to be a human in this situation." And you run that algorithm constantly, invisibly, at enormous cost. 

Looking back, the signs were always there, masked as mere "quirks." I didn't realize I had stims until I considered myself autistic. But then I remembered the insides of my mouth always having dead skin dangling off from me constantly chewing my cheeks. Every pencil I owned had its metal eraser back chewed flat. If I got anything weird or strong-smelling on my hands or sleeves, I would smell it constantly. Tactile issues were massive too — I hated having any amount of boogers in my nose, and I exclusively wore "wind pants" up until the 6th grade because I couldn't stand the texture of jeans or the feeling of the button. 

I even masked without knowing what masking was. Somewhere in elementary school, I heard a tip to "chew your food 20 times before swallowing." I took it literally and turned it into an unshakeable rule, counting the chews in my head at every meal — right up until I told my mom about it, and she questioned why I did it and told me it wasn't normal. So, I just stopped. I buried it. That's the algorithm at work: observe, adapt, hide. Until one day the system crashes.

## What's Actually Helping

I want to be honest here: I'm still figuring this out. These aren't prescriptions. They're things that have moved the needle for me personally so far.

### Noise-Canceling Headphones & Sensory Playlists

The single biggest environmental change has been having **active noise cancellation available at all times**. My Bose QuietComfort Pro 2 headphones have become less of a music delivery device and more of a neurological regulation tool. The active mode is incredible because it kind of equalizes all of the noises around you — so a sudden, piercing noise like a child's squeal gets leveled out to the same volume as their talking voice. Cutting out that unpredictable background sensory input reduces the cognitive load significantly enough that I can actually think.

Paired with this: a specific **sensory stim playlist**. Not background music for productivity. Music specifically chosen because of how it feels physically — bass, rhythm, specific frequencies — that functions more like a weighted blanket for the auditory system.

### The Eye Pressure Reset

This one sounds unusual but it works reliably for me. When I'm approaching overload, I'll lie down, close my eyes, and apply **light pressure to the outsides of both eyes** with my palms for about ten minutes.

I don't fully understand the mechanism. My best guess is that the gentle proprioceptive pressure, combined with removing visual input entirely, gives the nervous system a deliberate pause. Whatever the biology, it functions as a hard reset — I come back online with noticeably more capacity than I had before.

### Recognizing Social Energy Caps

This one is still the hardest to implement because it requires saying no to things, rescheduling, and communicating needs that the people around me didn't know I had.

But the fundamental insight is this: **social interaction costs me something real, and the budget is finite.** Most neurotypical people seem to have a recharging social battery — they get energy *from* being around people. Mine depletes. After a certain threshold, I don't just feel tired. I feel genuinely impaired — processing slows, emotional regulation drops, everything is harder.

Recognizing that cap exists — and taking it seriously rather than "pushing through" — is the actual work.

## The Gap Between Knowing and Having Support

One of the hardest things about late diagnosis is that you're doing all of this work — reading, adjusting, learning — while also trying to hold together a life that was built without any of these accommodations in mind.

I'm reading *Unmasking Autism* by Dr. Devon Price and working through *The Autism Burnout Recovery Workbook* by Dr. Vivian Ness. They're genuinely useful. But there's still a gap between what the books describe and what it looks like to actually implement it inside a career, a relationship, and parenthood simultaneously.

I don't have this figured out. But I'm learning what my nervous system actually needs instead of what I trained myself to tolerate. And that distinction is everything.
