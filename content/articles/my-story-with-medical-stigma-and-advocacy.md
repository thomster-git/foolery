I spent the first three decades of my life suffocating in the dark, and I didn't even know it.

For as long as I can remember, I snored. At ten years old, it was a joke at childhood sleepovers—friends complaining about the noise coming from my sleeping bag or throwing a pillow across the basement floor in the middle of the night to buy twenty minutes of quiet. You laugh it off as a kid because nobody thinks a noisy sleeper is in danger.

When you grow up in a body that has never had an uninterrupted night of oxygen, you have no baseline for what rested actually feels like. Your brain simply treats chronic exhaustion as normal life.

## What You Think Is Normal When You Never Sleep

Across fifteen years of adulthood—roughly 2010 through 2025—I assumed everyone around me felt the exact same leaden weight behind their eyes every afternoon and was just better at hiding it.

I genuinely thought it was normal to fight to keep your eyes open on a four-hour drive across Newfoundland on the Trans-Canada Highway. At 100 km/h, a vehicle covers 27.8 meters every second; a three-second microsleep means you have traveled 83.4 meters—nearly the length of a football field—completely unconscious behind the wheel. I would crack the driver's window to let freezing air hit my face, turn the radio up until the doors rattled, and drink another energy drink.

When I got home from work and sat down on the couch, I would fall asleep within three minutes. I didn't see any of it as a medical red flag. I thought it was just the price of working hard.

---

## Why I Put Off Getting Tested

The main reason I waited so long to mention it to a doctor was watching how the medical system had treated my father years earlier.

My dad fought the same exhaustion for years. Whenever he brought up his snoring and fatigue, the feedback he got back wasn't a sleep study—it was a lecture to *"just lose weight."* Watching that as a young adult taught me a bad lesson: if you walk into a clinic and tell them you snore and can't stay awake, you aren't going to get help; you're just going to get lectured.

Even during the periods of my adult life when I was in the best physical shape—lifting heavy, tracking every meal, and working out consistently—I was still exhausted every morning. My airway geometry didn't change when my gym numbers went up.

On top of that, I had an outdated picture of how sleep testing worked. I pictured a year-long waitlist to sleep in a cold hospital room covered in sixteen glued scalp wires while a technician watched through a window. Between the dread of a hospital sleep ward and the expectation of being brushed off, I kept quiet for fifteen years.

---

## Three Sensors in My Own Bed

When I finally brought it up with my family doctor, none of what I feared actually happened.

There was no overnight hospital stay. On a Friday afternoon I picked up a **Level 3 Home Sleep Apnea Testing (HSAT)** kit: an elastic chest belt to measure breathing effort, a nasal cannula for airflow, and a pulse oximeter taped to my finger. I wore it in my own bed over the weekend and dropped the case back at the hospital counter Monday morning.

The only difficult rule of the test was that I had to sleep flat on my back. If you have spent your whole life instinctively rolling onto your side to keep your airway from closing, lying flat on your back feels unnatural—which is the entire clinical point. Sleeping supine lets gravity pull the base of the tongue and soft palate straight back, showing the uncompensated worst-case behavior of your airway.

I dropped the kit off Monday expecting mild or borderline numbers.

---

## Sixty Times an Hour

A few days later, my doctor called me into her office and turned the report around on the desk.

| Sleep Study & Clinical Metric | Recorded Value | What It Means in Plain Language |
|---|---|---|
| **Apnea-Hypopnea Index (AHI)** | `60.0 events / hour` | Severe threshold is 30; my airway was closing off once every minute all night |
| **Lowest Blood Oxygen (SpO₂)** | `Mid-60s%` | Normal is 95–100%; drops below 78% carry major cardiac arrhythmia risk |
| **Test Type** | `Level 3 Take-Home HSAT` | Chest effort belt, nasal pressure cannula, and continuous pulse oximetry |
| **Provincial Licensing Rule** | `Mandatory MRD Reporting` | ≥4 hours/night on ≥70% of nights audited via cellular modem every 3 years |

An AHI over 30 is classified as severe. An **AHI of 60** meant that on average, once every sixty seconds all night long, my upper airway collapsed, airflow stopped, and my brain had to fire an adrenaline jolt to wake me up just enough to gasp for air before I could ever reach slow-wave or REM sleep.

Even more sobering was the oximetry trace. Healthy blood oxygen sits between 95% and 100%. By the time saturation drops into the **mid-60s%**, you are on the steep drop-off of the oxyhemoglobin dissociation curve (an arterial oxygen tension around 30–35 mmHg), well below the 78% nocturnal nadir that cardiology research (such as Gami et al. in the *Journal of the American College of Cardiology*, 2013) links to a sharply elevated risk of nocturnal arrhythmias and sudden cardiac arrest.

Because of the severity of the AHI and the highway drowsiness I had described, my doctor explained that under Newfoundland and Labrador's Highway Traffic Act medical reporting rules, she was legally required to notify the Motor Registration Division.

To keep my driver's license in good standing, my CPAP machine reports usage data over a built-in cellular modem, and every three years my compliance is audited against the provincial standard: **at least 4 hours of use per night on at least 70% of nights**. Honestly, when I think about how many times I drove across the province fighting heavy eyelids before I was diagnosed, I don't resent that requirement for a second.

## The First Morning & My Eyesight

The first night I slept with the CPAP mask on—letting gentle positive air pressure act as a pneumatic splint to hold my throat open—changed my life within forty-eight hours.

I woke up on the second morning and the heavy, bruised feeling behind my eyes was simply gone. For the first time in my adult memory, I drove down the highway without a single heavy blink. That evening I sat on the couch after supper, watched a movie from start to finish, and didn't nod off once.

Then came a second change that neither I nor my doctors had expected when I picked up the machine.

Back in 2010—fifteen years before my sleep study—I had begun dealing with recurring **macular edema**, a condition where fluid leaks through the tiny capillaries of the central retina, blurring and distorting central vision. After I started sleeping with CPAP every night and keeping my nocturnal oxygen stable at 96–98%, my eye specialist and I watched my macular swelling stabilize and clear on its own without requiring intraocular injections.

When you look at ophthalmic research, the link between the two makes complete physiological sense. The retina has one of the highest oxygen consumption rates of any tissue in the body. When nocturnal blood oxygen repeatedly plunges into the 60s, hypoxic retinal tissue stabilizes **Hypoxia-Inducible Factor 1-alpha (HIF-1α)**, which drives surges of **Vascular Endothelial Growth Factor (VEGF)**—the signaling protein that makes retinal capillaries leaky. In a 2012 clinical study published in *Retina*, **Dr. Rebecca H. Mason and colleagues** found an extraordinarily high rate of undiagnosed obstructive sleep apnea among patients with macular edema, noting that untreated nocturnal hypoxia continually re-triggers VEGF signaling while patients sleep. Keeping my airway open at night finally turned off the nightly hypoxic stress on my retinas.

## The Insulin Analogy

Obstructive sleep apnea is not a character flaw. In many patients, airway collapse is driven by craniofacial and anatomical geometry—jaw position, palate arch, soft-tissue crowding, and passive pharyngeal closing pressure—that no amount of willpower or gym sessions can remodel.

Telling a patient whose blood oxygen drops into the 60s every night to go fix their exhaustion with diet and exercise before treating their airway is like telling a patient with an blood sugar of 25 mmol/L to try jogging before you prescribe insulin. When your brain and heart are starved of oxygen sixty times an hour, your sleep architecture, cortisol, and metabolic hormones are already broken. You stabilize the airway first so the person can actually heal.

If you snore loudly, wake up with dry-mouth headaches, or think nodding off on the highway or the living room couch is just what adulthood feels like, ask your doctor for a weekend take-home sleep study. You don't realize how hard you were working just to breathe until you finally wake up rested.
