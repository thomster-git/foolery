There is a distinct satisfaction in bridging digital design with physical craftsmanship. While I've shared the operational details of EtchCentric in another article, the actual story behind my journey into 3D printing and laser engraving is really about learning how to make things, and learning some hard lessons about running a business along the way.

EtchCentric was a massive learning experience in what to do—and mostly what *not* to do—when trying to turn a hobby into a business. Frankly, business school would have helped. If I had known that a full-time job was right around the corner, I wouldn't have pushed so hard trying to get the business off the ground immediately. But at the time, I threw myself into it completely.

When it came time to pick a laser, I narrowed my options down and specifically chose the xTool S1 40W Diode Laser because I wanted to avoid the maintenance and complexities of CO2 lasers. The machine itself is a marvel, but it comes with a steep learning curve. For instance, I wish I had known early on just how difficult the feeding mechanic is to manage. The material needs to be much bigger than the actual cut area just so the rollers can grab and move it properly, and it has to be perfectly straight or everything drifts.

Despite the hurdles, I genuinely love the craft. Through EtchCentric, I produced a wide variety of items: layered wooden signs, intricately engraved pieces of wood, door corner decorations, firefighter identification tags, custom keychains, door tags, and coasters made of wood, slate, and mixed media. Among everything I created, the memorial acrylic pieces feel the most significant. Making something deeply personal for someone who has lost a loved one carries a weight and a meaning that standard products just don't have.

My approach to the artistic and maker side of this craft is heavily influenced by my background. I treat it the exact same way I'd treat a proctor compaction test or a material spec sheet: test, document, iterate. That engineering methodology is how I master the tools.

Whenever I get a new material, I run test grids. I set the laser power from 0 to 100, the speed from 0 to 600 mm/s, and just let it run. The result is a perfect reference grid showing different shades for engraving and different penetration levels for cutting. I keep these results and use them as my definitive reference for future work.

One of the key lessons I learned through this meticulous testing is that lines per inch (LPI) matters just as much as speed and power. If you run a material test at 100 LPI and find a setting you like, but then try to engrave your final piece at 300 LPI, the result will be significantly darker and might even char. That is the kind of critical nuance you rarely find explicitly warned about in the manual.

In the end, while the business side of EtchCentric didn't go exactly as planned, the engineering approach I applied to artistic creation left me with a deep appreciation for the craft, the tools, and the process of making.
