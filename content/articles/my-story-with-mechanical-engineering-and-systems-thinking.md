People sometimes ask how a Mechanical Engineering degree from Memorial University ended up leading into server architecture, network troubleshooting, and software development. To me, they never felt like different fields.

Back in my first student engineering term in early 2013 with the Industrial Outreach Group at MUN, we didn't work on abstract textbook problems. One week we were modeling and 3D-printing a one-handed shampoo dispenser attachment for clients with severe arthritis; the next week we were modifying a chocolate-coating machine for Chapman's Bakery to handle the viscosity change of a recipe using paraffin wax, and designing a can and bottle crusher for EverGreen Recycling that had to hit an exact volume-reduction target without exceeding structural crushing limits.

## Flow, Tolerances, and Bottlenecks

When you train in mechanical engineering, you learn to see every system in terms of conservation laws, tolerances, and load paths:
* If a fluid line loses pressure at the far end of a plant, you look for the constriction, the valve drop, or the pump curve mismatch.
* If a network crawls during morning shift login, you look for the 100 Mbps switch port, the storage IOPS queue, or the DNS timeout.

Software bugs and network outages feel mysterious only until you treat them like physical mechanisms. Every packet, thermal watt, and human handoff has to go somewhere—and once you trace the path from end to end, the bottleneck always reveals itself.
