> **Mechanical Engineering is not that different from software, IT, or systems design. The tools change. The thinking doesn't.**

---

## The Degree I Brute-Forced Through

I hold a Mechanical Engineering degree. It took more than academic aptitude to get it — it took a specific kind of processing that I now understand as part of being AuDHD.

High IQ masked a lot. Executive dysfunction that should have been catching up with me was compensated for by pattern recognition fast enough to understand concepts quickly, and working memory strong enough to perform under pressure when the pressure was immediate and concrete. Exams were manageable. Long-horizon project planning with ambiguous deadlines was where the real challenges lived.

But there was one project where the masking didn't matter, because my genuine interest and prior knowledge meant I was operating in a domain I actually understood deeply.

---

## Term 7: The RC Airplane Design Project

In Term 7, our class was tasked with designing and building an RC airplane. The project required fluid mechanics analysis, CAD modeling, and simulation using Computational Fluid Dynamics (CFD) software.

I had been interested in aviation since I was young — the aerodynamics, the control surfaces, the intersection of physics and flight. I had prior exposure to fluid mechanics as a subject I found genuinely engaging rather than something I was pushing through. And I was the most technically comfortable in our group with both the CFD software and the engineering analysis behind it.

Out of the entire class, our group produced the best results. More than that — I was able to explain *why* the simulations showed what they showed, what the airflow patterns meant, and what design changes they suggested. I understood the software at a conceptual level, not just an operational one. Our CAD model of the airplane was something I was genuinely proud of.

That project is one of the few academic memories I have where I wasn't compensating or masking. I was simply working in a domain that intersected with things I cared about, and the output reflected it.

The social dimension was a different story entirely. I never really fit in with my cohort. I had a hard time making friends in engineering. Every social gathering where the class met — study groups, project planning sessions, anything outside the structured classroom environment — felt like being in a room where everyone else had a social script I hadn't been given. I was there, participating, producing good work, and simultaneously completely alone in the crowd.

## Bottom-Up Thinking: The Engineering Habit That Carries Everywhere

The most transferable thing I took from engineering isn't a formula or a software package. It's a way of approaching problems.

I'm a **bottom-up thinker**.

That means when I'm designing something — a system, a process, a physical object, a software architecture — I start from the constraints and edge cases. I think about what happens when things go wrong before I think about what happens when they go right. I build from the foundation up, anticipating structural problems at the base before they become catastrophic failures at the top.

This is the engineering design approach. And it turns out to be equally useful in IT, in software architecture, and in thinking about the human systems that organizations run on.

When I walked into the server infrastructure at CFI, my instinct was to map the foundation first — what's actually here, what's undocumented, what's load-bearing — before touching anything. When I build software, I think about data structure and edge cases before I think about UI. When I'm troubleshooting a problem, I work from the hardware and network layer up, not from the application surface down.

That's Mechanical Engineering logic applied to digital systems. The tools change. The thinking doesn't.

## Aviation Checklists for Everyday Life

While I haven't fully implemented it yet, one specific application of systems thinking I think about constantly comes from my time getting my private pilot's license. 

In 2011, I did my flight training at Gander Flight Training through Air Cadets. One thing I immediately got used to—and found incredibly useful—were the laminated checklists for every critical phase of flight: start-up, shut-down, emergencies, etc. Some of them you had to memorize, but in a high-stress situation, it is incredibly easy to freeze up. Following the checklist every single time keeps you consistent. It prevents you from going absent-minded, forgetting to check if your elevator is working, and crashing on takeoff.

As silly as it sounds, I think this rigid, systemized approach would be profoundly helpful for my everyday life and executive dysfunction. Imagine a laminated "Getting Home from the Grocery Store" checklist:
- Bring kids in
- Put on a show (have a pre-selected list of options)
- Get kids a snack (something already prepared in the house)
- Start bringing in groceries
- Unbag groceries and place on the dining room table
- Put away pantry items
- Put away fridge items
- Throw away any old fridge food

It's the exact same logic. Take a chaotic, multi-step process with a high chance of distraction or failure, and engineer the friction out of it by removing the need to hold the steps in your working memory.

## Engineering → IT → Software: Why They're Not That Different

People sometimes find it surprising that someone with a Mechanical Engineering background ended up in IT coordination and then web development. From the inside, the path makes complete sense.

In my engineering group projects, I was usually the programmer — writing C to control circuit boards, microcontrollers, and motors. The logical structure of embedded code and the physical constraints of a mechanical system live in the same conceptual space. You're specifying behavior, managing state, accounting for failure modes.

That's also what good IT infrastructure design is. That's also what good software architecture is.

The surface vocabulary is different. The underlying habit of thought — systematic, constraint-aware, failure-anticipating, bottom-up — is the same everywhere I've applied it.

The degree was the formal container. The thinking was already there.
