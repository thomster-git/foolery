Billy Talent has been my favourite band for almost my entire life. I first started listening to them back in 2005 when I was twelve years old, right as my musical tastes were beginning to solidify. They quickly became the soundtrack to my adolescence, and their music has remained a steady, pulsing undercurrent in my life ever since.

My fandom ran deep early on. I vividly remember doing a school presentation on them in junior high. I poured over their history, researched every single member, and broke down the stories behind their songs. I wasn't just a casual listener; I wanted to understand the mechanics of the band and the people behind the punk-rock aggression.

The devotion paid off in grade 8, when my aunt—who was pregnant with her first child at the time—took me to see them live in St. John's. illScarlett opened the show. It was my very first concert, and the memory is etched into my brain. I can still picture exactly where we were sitting, the smell of the venue, the sheer volume of the guitars. It was a revelation. 

Since that first show, I've had the privilege of seeing Billy Talent multiple times, most notably at the Iceberg Alley Performance Tent. As I got older, I actively sought out tickets in the JAG Sky Bar VIP section. Let's be honest—I wanted the comfort. Private bars, accessible bathrooms, and decent food make a massive difference when you’re an adult trying to enjoy a punk show. 

But watching them over the years also meant watching them change. The first two times they played Iceberg Alley, their original drummer, Aaron Solowoniuk, was able to play a few songs each set. Any real fan knows about Aaron's battle with Multiple Sclerosis. It was incredibly moving to see him up there, pushing through. But by their third show at the tent, Aaron didn't play at all. It hit me unexpectedly hard. Watching a childhood hero struggle with an unrelenting illness is a harsh reminder of time passing. 

That final concert, though, stands out for another reason. I was in the VIP section, absolutely losing my mind—dancing, jumping, singing along to every word, completely lost in the music. Later on, a friend told me that the people standing near us were visibly irritated by how much fun I was having. They kept shooting me the stink eye for daring to rock out at a rock show. 

At the time, I didn't think much of it. But looking back on it now, especially through the lens of a recent AuDHD diagnosis, that moment feels profoundly different. For me, concerts aren't a background social event; they are supposed to be fully immersive, full-body experiences. I was doing exactly what my brain needed to do in that environment. 

In fact, it was at the Iceberg Alley Performance Tent, during a period of severe burnout in my life, that Billy Talent completely broke me open. I was standing there, listening to the band that had soundtracked my life from grade 8 into my thirties, and I just started crying. It wasn't sadness. It was pure, overwhelming gratitude. I was completely present, living in the moment, and genuinely so thankful to be seeing my favourite band. In a life full of noise and exhaustion, Billy Talent has been the one consistent chord that never fails to ring true.
