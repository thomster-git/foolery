> **I haven't reclaimed anything yet. And I want to be honest about that.**

---

## The YouTube Commentary

In Grade 12, I uploaded a Call of Duty gameplay commentary to YouTube.

I was doing what I'd seen other creators do — talking over my own footage, reacting, providing some kind of running narration. It was earnest. It was unpolished. It was me trying to participate in a format I found genuinely interesting.

The response I got wasn't from an anonymous internet audience. It was from people at school. Classmates who watched it, not because they found it through YouTube, but because someone found it and shared it specifically to laugh at it.

I became the punchline.

---

## The Pattern That Followed

That moment didn't happen in isolation. It was part of a longer pattern that shaped how I approached anything visible online.

**Facebook selfies**: People would question my sexual orientation in the comments. Not as genuine curiosity. As a way to mark the selfies as evidence of something wrong. The implication was clear: a boy doing this, posting this kind of thing, was suspect.

**Snapchat expressions**: People told me I made a weird face when snapping them. I couldn't really see or understand what they meant at the time. I didn't have proper glasses, so I was partially working blind — literally. What I've come to understand since is that I was probably raising my eyebrows high on my forehead, widening my eyes to see better given my prescription deficit. From the outside, it read as strange. No one told me that gently. They just said it was weird.

Every one of these was a data point my brain logged: *being visible online gets you hurt.*

---

## The Cost of Accumulated Feedback

Here's the thing about being autistic and receiving this kind of social feedback repeatedly: I catalogued it. Precisely. Not out of excessive sensitivity but out of the analytical habit that has always been how I process social experience. Each incident became a data point in an internal model I was building of what happens when you put yourself out there.

The model's conclusion was consistent: *ridicule is the predictable outcome.*

So I stopped. Not dramatically, not with a big decision, just quietly. I never posted a commentary again. I never seriously tried content creation again. The YouTube attempt and everything that came after it built a threshold I've been sitting below ever since.

I'm aware of what that cost me. I think about the version of me that kept going despite the Grade 12 response and what that trajectory might have looked like. But awareness of the cost doesn't automatically lower the threshold. The nervous system remembers even when the conscious mind decides to try again.

---

## Why Project Atlas Might Be Different

I'm not sure yet whether Project Atlas represents a genuine reclaiming of public voice or something more protected.

The format is different. It's not a video that gets shared and mocked in a school hallway. It's a written knowledge garden, structured and considered, where the content lives behind a domain I control and the context is established before anything else.

But the deeper thing is this: I'm writing from inside my own diagnostic understanding now. Knowing I'm autistic means knowing *why* I make a face on Snapchat, why my selfies registered as strange, why my YouTube commentary sounded earnest in a way that was read as naive. It doesn't make the old experiences hurt less. But it changes the interpretation. The weirdness wasn't a failure. It was a misread — by people who didn't have the right frame, including me.

Writing here, with that frame, feels different from what I was doing at eighteen.

I'm not claiming I've reclaimed anything. I'm saying I'm trying again, more carefully, with a better understanding of what went wrong the first time and why.

That feels like enough for now.
