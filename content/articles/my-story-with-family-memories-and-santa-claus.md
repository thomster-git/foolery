> **Reflecting on financial empathy as a child, discovering old family photos, and the heavy burden of neurodivergent hyper-empathy.**

Children process complex emotions long before they have the vocabulary to articulate them. For neurodivergent children, this often manifests as "hyper-empathy"—an intense, almost crushing absorption of the emotional atmospheres around them, even when the context isn't fully understood. Looking back at key moments with my family, I realize how deeply I absorbed these ambient emotions and internalized them as immense guilt and personal responsibility.

## Grade 6 & The Santa Claus Realization

Growing up, we weren't well off financially. I knew this early on. It wasn't explicitly stated, but it lived in the margins of everyday life—my parents gently getting me to ration my ketchup, the careful calculations in the grocery aisle, and just a general ambient feeling of knowing we weren't as comfortable as other kids in my class. 

For me, the concept of Santa Claus wasn't just about magic or reindeer. Santa was the vital emotional wall between feeling guilty for asking for toys and facing the harsh reality of our finances. 

I actually knew Santa wasn't real long before my parents told me. I had deduced it logically from discrepancies in handwriting and logic flaws pointed out by kids at school. Furthermore, two years before the official "talk," I was awake late on Christmas Eve and distinctly heard and saw my parents putting presents under the tree. 

I kept this knowledge fiercely guarded. 

When I was in Grade 6, my mom finally sat me down to tell me the truth. She felt an urgent need to tell me so I wouldn’t get bullied in middle school for still believing in a childish myth. When she gently broke the news, I broke down crying hysterically. To this day, I think she assumes I was crying because the magic was gone, or because I felt betrayed that I had been lied to.

But what really hurt, what truly shattered my nervous system in that moment, was that my plausible deniability was gone. My ability to be willfully ignorant of the severe financial impact Christmas had on my family was erased. All of these expensive presents that "Santa" gave me didn't come from a magical infinite workshop—they came from my struggling, exhausted parents. 

Believing in Santa allowed me to disconnect my childhood desires from their tangible hardship. Once that barrier was removed, the hyper-empathy crashed down on me. The guilt of every toy I had ever asked for hit me all at once.

## The Prom Photo Album

This profound internalization of guilt wasn't an isolated incident. Another pivotal moment occurred when I found my father's old high school prom photo album tucked away in a closet. 

Flipping through the pages, looking at the pictures, I completely broke down crying again. When my mother found me sobbing over the album, she naturally assumed I was upset seeing my father with a different woman before they had met. It was a logical neurotypical assumption.

But my neurodivergent reality was far darker. I was reacting viscerally to the expression on my father's face in those old photos—a look of pure, unburdened joy, free from the chronic stress, financial pressure, and sensory overstimulation that I associated with adulthood. 

Knowing that I was born out of wedlock, my young, analytical mind connected the dots in the most brutal way possible. I interpreted the joy in that vintage photo as empirical evidence that my parents would have been far happier, far more carefree, and far less stressed if I had never existed. I absorbed the ambient stress of our household and concluded that I was the root cause of it all.

## Processing the Past

Processing these early emotional connections through the lens of adult self-awareness and an AuDHD diagnosis has been essential. It helps explain the intense emotional dysregulation and the tendency to assume extreme personal responsibility for the emotions of those around me. Unlearning this childhood guilt is an ongoing process, but recognizing the mechanics of hyper-empathy is the first step toward building authentic, unburdened relationships with my family today.
