If you want to spark a debate faster than asking what the best season of The Office is, ask a group of parents about potty training. Everyone has a strategy, and everyone is convinced theirs is the only one that won't result in a teenager in diapers.

But after seeing two very different approaches play out in real-time, I’m pretty sure I’ve figured out what works—and what definitely doesn’t.

Let's look at the tale of two kids, both born in 2022.

Our son, born at the end of February '22, was fully potty trained just before his third birthday. On the flip side, we have close friends with a daughter born just a few months later, at the end of October. She’s approaching four, and let's just say... the training wheels are very much still on.

So, what's the difference? The method.

### The Candy Trap

Our friends have been using the classic "candy as positive reinforcement" method. And I get it. When you are staring down the barrel of your 500th diaper change, handing over an M&M for a successful trip to the porcelain throne seems like a totally reasonable trade.

But here’s the catch: they’ve been doing this since at least the summer of last year. That means they started before she turned three, and over a year later, she is in the exact same spot.

Why? Because she hasn't learned to use the potty; she’s learned how to run a highly effective extortion racket.

As it turns out, the psychology backs this up. It comes down to extrinsic vs. intrinsic motivation. Extrinsic rewards (like candy, stickers, or toys) are great for getting a kid to do a one-off task they hate. But when used long-term for a developmental milestone, they can actually kill intrinsic motivation. According to Self-Determination Theory, when a child relies purely on an external reward, the reward becomes the only reason for the behavior. They stop caring about the pride of being a "big kid" or listening to their body's signals.

Plus, candy has diminishing returns. Eventually, that single M&M isn't enough, and suddenly you're negotiating a full-sized Snickers bar for a number two. The child learns to game the system, and the potty becomes a transactional battleground.

### The "Bare-Bottom" Reality Check

So, what did we do with our son? We went the opposite route. We used the "no underwear, no diaper" trick.

For a few days, we just let him roam the house with nothing on below the waist. Was it a bit chaotic? Yes. Did it require a surplus of paper towels and enzyme cleaner? Absolutely.

But here's why it works so incredibly well: natural consequences.

When a kid is wearing a pull-up or a diaper, they don't really feel it when they go. It's a built-in safety net. But when they are completely bare-bottomed, pee running down their leg is surprising, uncomfortable, and immediate. The accidents are basically a flashing neon sign that says, "Hey! You waited too long!"

They catch on remarkably fast because they want to avoid making a mess and feeling uncomfortable. It forces their brain to connect the internal physical sensation of "I need to go" with the external result, without needing a sugary bribe to bridge the gap. We didn't need to reward him; the reward was simply not having wet legs. By the time he turned three, he was fully trained.

### The Ultimate Proof: The Younger Sibling

If you need any more proof that a natural, low-pressure environment works better than bribery, just look at our daughter. She isn't even two yet, and we haven't formally started training her at all.

Yet, she already occasionally uses the potty to pee and poop. Why? Because she sees it happening naturally in the house. It's not a transaction, it's just what people do. She's developing her own intrinsic motivation simply by being in an environment where using the toilet is a normal part of the day, not a carnival game where you win a prize.

### The Verdict

Potty training is tough, and I don't blame any parent for grabbing whatever tools they have at their disposal to get through it. But if you find yourself a year into a candy-based strategy with no end in sight, it might be time to cut off the sugar supply and hide the pull-ups.

It might be a messy weekend, but trust me—it beats negotiating with a tiny candy cartel for the next year.
