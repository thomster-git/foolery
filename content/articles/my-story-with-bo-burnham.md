I first discovered Bo Burnham way back in 2006 on YouTube. I was just a kid, spending hours scrolling through the early days of the platform, and something about his rapid-fire, musical comedy hooked me instantly. I looked up to him; he was this prodigy doing exactly what I wished I could do. I even made a point to tune in to YouTube Live in 2007 specifically to see his performance—a digital pilgrimage for a teenager in the mid-2000s. 

My fandom was so earnest that when it came time to pick a yearbook "song" for my graduating class, I tried submitting "High School Party." Unfortunately for me, my teachers actually looked up the lyrics beforehand and swiftly vetoed the idea. 

In grade 12, I watched *Words Words Words*. At the time, the rapid-fire Shakespeare references went over my head. But then we studied *Hamlet* that same year, and suddenly the joke clicked. It felt like an inside joke I was finally allowed to understand. Looking back, I feel most connected to that earlier era of his career—the days of Derrick Comedy collaborations, the YouTube "camp" era before he hit mainstream virality. 

But as I grew up, Bo did too. When *what.* came out, I was fully on board. That special marked a shift; he started getting more creative and personal, stepping back from the purely performative shtick. Before that, "Art is Dead" was the only song that felt truly meaningful. But *what.* opened the door to a more introspective Bo. During this time, I was also watching his Vine content, trying to absorb whatever snippet of his creativity he put out into the world.

The shift deepened with *Make Happy*. This special is inextricably linked to a difficult chapter in my own life—it came out the exact same year I began receiving treatment for depression. One of the tracks, "Kill Yourself," became a literal earworm for me. It got stuck in my head so severely that I had to actively avoid listening to it or watching the special too much. It wasn't a casual listen anymore; the material was cutting too close to the bone.

When he directed *Eighth Grade*, I skipped it entirely. I had a bad relationship with social media, and I knew the film would hit too close to home. Even now, years later, I still haven't seen it. It’s harder to find on streaming, so I'm not actively avoiding it as much as I once was, but the initial hesitation was real.

Then came *Inside*. Like it did for many people, it resonated with me deeply. It furthered the theme of his work becoming increasingly intimate and personal. The performative mask had slipped, revealing the raw anxiety underneath—something I understood all too well. 

Today, Bo Burnham's music has become a sort of stim tool for me. Listening to him makes me genuinely happy. It’s a familiar voice that has unknowingly followed me through adolescence, into my early twenties, and now lingers as I navigate my thirties. Over the years, I've taught myself to play several of his songs on guitar and piano: "Goodbye," "That's How the World Works," and "The Chicken" from the *Inside* outtakes. I think I know "What's Funny" from *what.*, and I’m currently trying to nail the intricate fingering nuances of "That Funny Feeling."

Sometimes I entertain the idea of putting together a Bo Burnham tribute or cover show. I have a whole concept for it. But realistically, I don't think I’d ever be able to muster the courage or advocate for myself enough to make it happen. Still, the fact that the desire is there says it all. Bo Burnham is the through-line of my digital life, a parasocial relationship that has grown, evolved, and endured for twenty years.
