Almost everyone agrees that changing our clocks twice a year is a broken ritual that needs to end.

Ask ten people on the street if they want to get rid of the spring and fall time shifts, and nine of them will say yes before you finish the sentence. Nobody enjoys resetting the microwave and oven clocks, wrestling tired kids onto a shifted bedtime schedule, or dragging through two weeks of March grogginess because a century-old wartime policy says we should pretend it is an hour later than the sun says it is.

In the fall of 2026, the debate came to a head across western and central Canada—with British Columbia, Alberta, and Manitoba (following the Yukon's 2020 switch, while Ontario's *Time Amendment Act, 2020* sits waiting on Quebec and New York) pushing toward year-round **Permanent Daylight Saving Time**.

Ending the twice-yearly clock switch is the right goal. Locking onto Daylight Saving Time in the fall is the wrong clock.

By refusing to fall back in November instead of waiting until March to simply stop "springing forward," governments confused *hating the clock change* with *wanting permanent summer hours in the middle of a Canadian winter*.

## How Loaded Surveys & "Spring Forward" Manufactured a False Mandate

Whenever politicians defend choosing Permanent Daylight Saving Time over Permanent Standard Time, they point to public consultations—most famously British Columbia’s summer 2019 online survey, where **93% of 223,273 respondents** checked the box to move to permanent Daylight Saving Time.

Whenever an online poll returns 93% agreement on a policy trade-off, the first thing you should check is how the question was written and when it was asked.

### 1. The Missing Option on the Ballot
In B.C.’s June–July 2019 survey and Alberta’s October 2021 referendum, voters were never asked a clean two-part question:
1. *Do you want to stop changing clocks twice a year?*
2. *If yes, should our year-round clock be Standard Time (aligned with solar noon) or Daylight Saving Time (shifted an hour ahead)?*

Instead, Alberta’s 2021 ballot bundled both ideas into a single yes-or-no question:
> *"Do you want Alberta to adopt year-round Daylight Saving Time, which is summer hours, eliminating the need to change our clocks twice a year?"*

(Even with that wording, Albertans narrowly voted it down **`50.2% No` to `49.8% Yes`** in 2021.)

Meanwhile, B.C.’s 2019 survey pitted keeping the twice-yearly clock switch against adopting permanent Daylight Saving Time—leaving **Permanent Standard Time** off the ballot as a primary choice. When you tell people that the only way to stop changing their clocks is to check the box labeled "Permanent Daylight Saving Time," 93% aren't voting for a 9:30 AM winter sunrise. They are voting to stop changing their clocks.

### 2. Why People Actually Hate the Clock Change
Nobody wakes up on the first Sunday of November angry that they just got an extra hour of sleep. What people hate is the **March "Spring Forward" shift**—the Sunday morning where the clock jumps from 2:00 AM to 3:00 AM and steals an hour of sleep.

That single lost hour in March leaves a measurable trail in hospital and highway data:
* In a study of Michigan hospital admissions published in *Open Heart* (Sandhu et al., 2014; building on Janszky & Ljung in the *New England Journal of Medicine*, 2008), acute myocardial infarctions (heart attacks) jumped **24% on the Monday immediately following the Spring Forward transition**.
* An analysis of twenty years of U.S. traffic records published in *Current Biology* (Fritz et al., 2020) found a **6% spike in fatal motor-vehicle crashes** during the workweek after Spring Forward, concentrated in the morning commute.

When you poll people in the middle of summer—or right after they have been burned by the March clock jump—their answer is driven by two thoughts: *"I never want to lose that hour of sleep in March again,"* and *"We're on Daylight Saving Time right now and the weather is warm, so let's just leave it here."*

Even the name is a century-old marketing trick. Calling `UTC + 1` *"Daylight Saving Time"* makes it sound like the government is printing extra sunshine, while *"Standard Time"* sounds like the base trim on a fleet pickup truck.

And as soon as winter arrives, that summer polling illusion evaporates. In the **Angus Reid Institute's** tracking of the permanent-DST debate across B.C., Alberta, and Manitoba, support for permanent Daylight Saving Time in Alberta fell 14 percentage points once autumn darkness set in (dropping from 47% down to **33%**), with a **44% plurality** favoring **Permanent Standard Time** instead.

Look at what that one-hour rightward shift does to winter mornings and summer evenings:

### What Winter Mornings Look Like
Under **Standard Time**, Canadian winter mornings are already close to the bone—sunrise happens between `7:46 AM` in St. John's and `8:48 AM` in Edmonton, right as kids are walking to school and morning traffic is on the highway.

Under **Permanent Daylight Saving Time**, you push winter sunrise back a full hour:
* **`9:05 AM`** in Vancouver
* **`9:24 AM`** in Winnipeg
* **`9:36 AM`** in Calgary
* **`9:48 AM`** in Edmonton

For three months of the year, elementary school kids wait at bus stops and cross icy intersections in dark night, and outdoor crews and morning commuters work for two to three hours after waking up before the sun clears the horizon. In exchange for three months of dark 9:30 AM mornings, winter sunset moves from `4:20 PM` to `5:20 PM`—when most daytime staff are still sitting in traffic on the way home anyway.

### What Summer Evenings Look Like on Standard Time
The biggest worry people voice about **Permanent Standard Time** is: *"Won't we lose our summer evenings?"*

Look at June 21 on Standard Time across those same six cities:
* **St. John's (`47.6°N`):** Sunrise `4:03 AM` · Sunset **`8:02 PM`** (vs. `9:02 PM` DST)
* **Toronto (`43.7°N`):** Sunrise `4:36 AM` · Sunset **`8:03 PM`** (vs. `9:03 PM` DST)
* **Vancouver (`49.3°N`):** Sunrise `4:07 AM` · Sunset **`8:22 PM`** (vs. `9:22 PM` DST)
* **Winnipeg (`49.9°N`):** Sunrise `4:20 AM` · Sunset **`8:41 PM`** (vs. `9:41 PM` DST)
* **Calgary (`51.0°N`):** Sunrise `4:22 AM` · Sunset **`8:54 PM`** (vs. `9:54 PM` DST)
* **Edmonton (`53.5°N`):** Sunrise `4:04 AM` · Sunset **`9:07 PM`** (vs. `10:07 PM` DST)

Because Canada sits at a high northern latitude, summer days are naturally 15.5 to 17+ hours long. On **Permanent Standard Time**, the June sun still doesn't set until between `8:02 PM` and `9:07 PM`, with bright civil twilight lasting another 45 to 55 minutes past sunset. You still get four full hours of bright post-work daylight every evening—and bedrooms actually cool down and grow dark in time for kids and early-shift workers to fall asleep before `10:30 PM`, instead of staring at a `10:07 PM` sun in Edmonton.

## Solar Noon & Why Morning Light Matters

Having lived for thirty years with [undiagnosed sleep apnea](/articles/my-story-with-medical-stigma-and-advocacy.html) before getting on CPAP, I learned the hard way that sleep isn't a luxury you can shortchange without paying for it elsewhere. Walking out the front door here in Newfoundland on a dark winter morning to scrape frost off a windshield while your brain hasn't seen a ray of sunlight makes it obvious how heavily the human body depends on dawn cues.

That is why the major sleep and chronobiology societies—the **American Academy of Sleep Medicine** (Rishi et al., *Journal of Clinical Sleep Medicine*, 2020/2024), the **Canadian Sleep Society (CSS)**, the **Canadian Society for Chronobiology (CSC)**, and the **Society for Research on Biological Rhythms** (Roenneberg et al., 2019)—all formally oppose Permanent Daylight Saving Time and endorse **Permanent Standard Time**.

Standard Time is defined so that **12:00 PM on the clock roughly aligns with Solar Noon**—the moment the sun is highest in the sky. Daylight Saving Time shifts the social clock an hour ahead of the sun (`Solar Noon ≈ 1:00 PM` to `1:30+ PM`).

Left without sunlight cues, the human master circadian pacemaker in the hypothalamus (the suprachiasmatic nucleus, or SCN) free-runs slightly longer than 24 hours—averaging about `24.15 to 24.2 hours` (Czeisler et al., *Science*, 1999). To stay anchored to a 24-hour day, your brain needs two daily light signals:
1. **Morning blue-spectrum sunlight** hitting retinal ganglion cells shortly after waking to shut off residual melatonin, trigger morning alertness, and start the 14-to-16-hour countdown to tonight's sleep.
2. **Evening darkness** so melatonin can rise naturally before a 6:00 AM alarm.

Permanent Daylight Saving Time gets both signals backward—depriving the brain of morning light all winter and pushing bright sunlight past 10:00 PM in the summer. Economists and epidemiologists who have studied populations living on the western edges of timezones—where sunrise and solar noon occur an hour later by clock time, the exact year-round equivalent of permanent DST—have documented shorter average sleep duration, higher rates of obesity, diabetes, and cardiovascular disease, lower wages (Giuntella & Mazzonna, *Journal of Health Economics*, 2019), and elevated cancer incidence (Gu et al., *Cancer Epidemiology, Biomarkers & Prevention*, 2017).

## Every Country That Tried Permanent DST Rolled It Back

We don't have to guess how permanent winter Daylight Saving Time plays out, because three major jurisdictions already tried it and reversed course:

* **The United States (1974):** During the 1973 OPEC oil embargo, the U.S. Congress enacted a two-year trial of year-round Daylight Saving Time starting January 6, 1974. In December 1973, **79% of Americans** told pollsters they supported the move. Once parents spent January sending their children to school in pitch darkness—and after eight fatal morning school-age pedestrian crashes in Florida—public approval plunged to **42%** within six weeks. By August 1974, the U.S. House of Representatives voted **383 to 16** to abort the experiment early and return to Standard Time that October.
* **The United Kingdom (1968–1971):** Britain tested year-round summer time (*British Standard Time*, `UTC+1`) for three years. In northern Scotland—at latitudes matching Edmonton and northern B.C.—winter sunrise didn't happen until `10:00 AM`. In December 1970, the House of Commons voted **366 to 81** to end the trial.
* **Russia (2011–2014):** Russia abolished clock changes in 2011 by locking onto permanent Daylight Saving Time. After three winters of dark mornings and sliding poll numbers, Russia reversed the law in October 2014, moved clocks back one final time, and has stayed on **permanent year-round Standard Time** ever since.
* **Saskatchewan (1966–Present):** Closer to home, Saskatchewan has kept a single clock all year for sixty years without switching twice a year.

## Wait Until Spring, Then Stop Touching the Clock

When you want to retire a broken twice-a-year process, *when* you stop matters.

If you lock the clocks in November, you take away the only clock change people actually enjoy (getting an extra hour of sleep in the fall) and immediately drop school kids and commuters into four months of 9:15 AM to 9:48 AM sunrises.

If instead you let the clocks **fall back to Standard Time in November** one last time, everyone gets their hour of sleep, 12:00 PM returns to Solar Noon, and Canadians get every available minute of winter morning light. Then, when the second Sunday of March arrives, **you simply do nothing**. Nobody loses an hour of sleep, nobody deals with a Monday-morning circadian hangover, and within two weeks the spring equinox naturally gives us both bright mornings and 8:30 PM summer evenings.

End the clock changes—just wait until spring and stay on Standard Time.
