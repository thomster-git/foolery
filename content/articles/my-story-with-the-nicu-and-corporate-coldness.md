The most painful experience with healthcare occurred during the birth of my son.

I’ve always been pretty good at keeping it together during very high-stress events, holding onto my emotions until the crisis resolves. For instance, when my cat got out one evening, I remained perfectly calm and collected during the search, but the moment we found him, I broke down crying because I was finally safe to be vulnerable. That same crisis processing carried me through the delivery room.

During delivery, my son suffered **shoulder dystocia**, resulting in a collapsed lung. When he was born, he came out completely blue and did not change color. He was immediately wheeled out of the delivery room without explanation.

```
Delivery Room: Shoulder Dystocia & Collapsed Lung
  └──> 2 Hours of Agonizing Silence (No Updates)
        └──> Medical Intervention: 10 mL Air Syringed from Chest
              └──> Transfer to NICU in St. John's
```

My partner and I were left waiting in agonizing silence for **nearly two hours** before a doctor finally returned to inform us what was happening. The physician had to manually syringe 10 mL of trapped air from his chest before transferring him to the Neonatal Intensive Care Unit (NICU) in St. John's.

As scared, exhausted first-time parents, our experience with the NICU nursing staff was deeply distressing:
- **Disrespect & Scolding**: After being instructed by doctors to wait until morning rounds before entering, nurses scolded us upon arrival, snapping *"Baby needs to eat, you know."*
- **Lack of Empathy**: When our son developed jaundice, nursing staff pressured us to switch from breastfeeding to formula without stating it directly, showing zero empathy for my partner's struggles.
- **The 2oz Bottle**: At one point, a nurse explicitly told us she would find it hard to get a full 2oz bottle into him. When we eventually got transferred to a less critical room, we managed to get him to finish a 2oz bottle. When that same nurse came by and asked how he was doing, I smugly replied, "Good, we just got him to finish a 2-ounce bottle." She had nothing to say and we didn't see her for the rest of the visit.

Looking back, I think it would be easier to advocate for ourselves now by disclosing that I'm autistic to request extra supports, but the trauma of navigating that crisis without help is still something I'm getting over.

## Corporate Coldness in the Hospital Room

Compounding the NICU trauma, **five days prior to my son's birth**, Canada Fluorspar Inc. (CFI)—the mining company where I worked as IT Coordinator—suddenly entered receivership.

I lost my job overnight with zero notice, zero severance, and no closure.

While sitting in the hospital room holding my newborn son in the NICU, I received an email from CFI Human Resources claiming they had tried to contact me (despite zero missed calls) and informing me that I needed to come to the mine site to collect my personal belongings.

The complete absence of empathy from corporate management during a family medical emergency was appalling. 

Navigating parental trauma and a sudden layoff simultaneously has taught me that massive systems—whether healthcare or corporate HR—cannot function on algorithms and policies alone. They require human empathy. First-time parents in crisis deserve dignity, respect, and clear communication. And employees deserve to be treated like human beings, not line items on a receivership spreadsheet.
