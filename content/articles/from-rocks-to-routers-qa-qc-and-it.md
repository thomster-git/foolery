A question I get asked a lot is: *How does a Mechanical Engineering graduate doing QA/QC work end up building IT networks?*

For a while, my day job literally involved rocks. I was working in St. Lawrence (my fiancée’s hometown, which is why I applied for the job in the first place), doing QA/QC work on aggregate. This meant placing aggregate in specific layers and constantly monitoring particle size, placing techniques, and compaction levels. 

It was highly structured, detail-oriented work. 

But I didn't make the jump to IT because I suddenly discovered a passion for Ethernet cables. I made the jump because, at my core, I am a **problem solver**, not just someone interested in mechanical engineering. 

## The Common Thread of Systems

I am an incredibly fast learner. More importantly, as long as I understand *how* something works and what its ultimate *purpose* is, I can build, fix, or optimize it. 

The leap from Mechanical Engineering to IT infrastructure isn't actually that far if you view the world through the lens of systems. A computer network is just digital plumbing. Routers are valves directing flow, bandwidth is just flow rate, and firewalls are essentially complex filtration systems. 

When you understand the fundamental physics of a system—whether it's monitoring the compaction of rock layers to ensure structural integrity, or monitoring network traffic to ensure data integrity—the problem-solving methodologies are identical. You look for the bottleneck, you identify the root cause, and you design a workflow to prevent it from happening again.

## Taking Capabilities for Granted

Long before I was running corporate servers, I built my own computer at home. For a long time, I took that for granted. I assumed everyone understood how these components talked to each other. 

Because of my AuDHD, I often perceive my own abilities differently. To me, I'm not that exceptional. I'm just looking at the system and pointing out the obvious flaws. I've often felt that others don't view me as exceptional either, simply because I don't fit the neurotypical mold of what a "corporate leader" or "tech visionary" looks or acts like. 

But looking back at the trajectory—from analyzing rocks in St. Lawrence to designing $34,000 white-box server architectures that save companies tens of thousands of dollars—I realize that this specific way of viewing the world is a massive advantage. I might not always think I'm exceptional, but I know I am capable of extraordinary things. 

When you stop caring about the labels (Mechanical Engineer, IT Consultant, QA Tech) and just focus on solving the problem in front of you, you'd be amazed at what you can build.
