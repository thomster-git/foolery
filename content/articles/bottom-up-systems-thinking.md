## The Flaw of Top-Down Assumptions

There is a lot of top-down thinking going around in corporate environments and management structures. It usually looks like this: a metric is missed, a machine breaks, or a process fails. The immediate assumption, flowing from the top down, is often human error or operator incompetence.

But if a piece of machinery keeps breaking and you just assume it's operator error, you're entirely overlooking the foundational possibility of it being something else. Is the piece of equipment undersized for the job? Is there a systemic flaw in the workflow?

There is almost a shame attached to people who are bottom-up thinkers — the people who pause and ask, *"Wait, why is it doing this in the first place?"*

## The Engineering Habit

Bottom-up systems thinking is an engineering habit. It means when you design or troubleshoot something — a system, a process, a physical object, or a software architecture — you start from the constraints and the edge cases. 

You think about what happens when things go wrong before you think about what happens when they go right. You build from the foundation up, anticipating structural problems at the base before they become catastrophic failures at the top.

## The Power of the "Why"

This philosophy extends directly to how rules and processes are communicated to teams. Standardized workflows and rules are often handed down with zero context.

I realized the importance of the "why" while working at a mine regarding safety rules. I always thought it was incredibly stupid and redundant that wheel chocks needed to be placed on light-duty vehicles in the parking lot. It made absolutely no sense to me. 

But then the mine manager explained the *bottom-up* reasoning: the physical act of removing the chocks forces the driver to do a mandatory 360-degree walkaround of the vehicle before getting in. It wasn't about the vehicle rolling away; it was an engineered behavioral failsafe. Once the "why" was explained, I thought it was brilliant.

When you understand the foundation, the rules make sense. When you build from the bottom up, the systems don't collapse.
