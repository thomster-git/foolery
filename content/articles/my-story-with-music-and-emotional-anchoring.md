> **Before kids, I listened to between 10,000 and 150,000 minutes of music every year. Then that number hit zero. I didn't realize how much I needed it until it was gone.**

---

## The Number That Says Everything

Every year, music streaming platforms tell you how many minutes you listened.

Mine used to be embarrassing in the best way. 10,000 minutes in quieter years. 150,000 in the ones where music was the background frequency of my entire life.

Then the number dropped to zero.

Not because I chose to stop listening. Not because my taste changed. But because the space that music occupied got replaced — first by the demands of new parenthood, and then by Autistic Burnout that stripped my sensory tolerance down to nearly nothing. Children's songs moved in. Everything else moved out.

I didn't know how important that was until the silence stretched long enough that I noticed it.

---

## The Chronological Anchors

Looking back at my life, my musical eras map perfectly to my emotional transitions. It's a chronological timeline of anchoring:

- **Early Childhood:** **Great Big Sea** was my first favorite band. I had *Play!* on cassette, and to this day it remains a deeply nostalgic, feel-good album for me.
- **Grade 4 (Fitting In):** I bought **Usher's** CD because I actually liked it — but so did all the girls in my grade. I also spent time trying to record **Eminem** off the radio because my parents wouldn't let me buy CDs with Parental Advisory stickers.
- **Grade 6:** **Simple Plan** became my anchor, specifically their first two albums. 
- **Grade 7 (The Punk Awakening):** I learned that Simple Plan "wasn't cool," so I pivoted to **Green Day**. I bought *Dookie* because miraculously it didn't have a warning sticker (until I read the lyric book on the drive home and quickly hid it). I also burned two copies of *American Idiot* — one live version, and one studio version that I accidentally downloaded as the censored version, which made me hate the whole thing.
- **The Billy Talent Era:** Then I found **Billy Talent**, and the rest was history.
- **Post-Secondary (2011+):** A friend introduced me to **Tokyo Police Club** and **Cage the Elephant**. Like most bands I love, their first two albums resonate with me the most. I still prefer listening to *Champ* front to back.
- **The Electronic/Hip-Hop Shift:** Mixed into all of this, I got heavily into dubstep, **Skrillex** (including his early Sonny Moore days and Jack Ü), and eventually the first three albums of **Post Malone**.

These weren't just phases. These albums were the literal soundtrack to my nervous system trying to figure out how to exist in the world at those specific ages.

---

## The Billy Talent Concert

The last time I saw Billy Talent in concert, I quietly cried to myself at one point.

Not dramatically. Not in a way anyone noticed. The two people I was with didn't see it. It wasn't grief exactly — it was the opposite. An overwhelming, almost physical sense of *rightness*. Being in that room, with that sound, feeling the bass in my chest and the crowd around me in the dark — something in my nervous system registered it as a homecoming.

I didn't fully understand why it hit so hard until I started understanding what Autistic Burnout had actually taken from me.

Music had always been one of my primary regulation tools, even when I didn't have the vocabulary to call it that. The rhythm. The volume. The specific frequencies of a distorted guitar. These aren't just aesthetically pleasing to me — they function more like environmental anchors, things that help my nervous system orient and settle. Live music amplifies all of this, turning it into something full-body and immersive.

Standing in that room, that was the first time in a long time my nervous system had been given everything it was asking for simultaneously: predictable structure (the set list, the familiar songs), intense sensory input that I was *choosing* rather than being subjected to, and a crowd of people all focused in the same direction, not requiring anything social from me.

I was overwhelmed in the good way. And I cried.

---

## What Music Actually Does

I didn't know until recently that what I was describing was stimming.

The way certain songs feel physically different from others. The way a particular bass line or drum pattern seems to organize my internal state. The way I've always listened to the same albums on rotation rather than constantly discovering new things — the predictability itself being part of the value.

Music is an auditory stim. For a lot of autistic people it serves the same neurological function as physical movement, pressure, or repetitive tactile input. It regulates the nervous system. It provides a consistent, trustworthy sensory environment in a world that is often neither.

This reframes a lot of things about my relationship with music. The intensity. The emotional response to live shows. The way certain artists (Billy Talent, Bo Burnham, Donald Glover) aren't just music I enjoy but reference points I return to across years of my life.

---

## The Loss and the Return

The burnout years were years of flattened sensory experience. Things that used to bring intense pleasure — music, food, creative projects — stopped generating the response they used to. This is one of the hallmarks of burnout: the things that used to help don't reach you anymore.

The music coming back is a recovery signal I'm paying attention to.

Not fully back. Not at 150,000 minutes a year. But audible again. Present again. Something I can reach for and feel when I find the right song in the right moment.

That concert was one of those moments. And the fact that I cried at it — alone, quietly, surrounded by people who didn't notice — feels less like weakness now than it did at the time. It feels like something working correctly after a long period of not working.
