Most organizations approach problem-solving backwards: they buy a shiny new piece of software and then try to force their business to fit its constraints. 

I do the exact opposite. 

It doesn’t matter how technically superior a piece of software is if the end users refuse to use it. I’ve seen companies purchase massive, expensive software licenses only for the entire implementation to fall apart because of negative backlash and poor planning. 

In one instance, a company I worked for paid roughly $20,000 for a Slack enterprise deployment, purely chasing the "next big thing" without considering the implications. They did this just four months before being thrown into government receivership. Microsoft Teams was already included in our environment and worked perfectly fine for our needs, but the desire to micromanage trumped all rational planning. 

## Mapping the Human Process

My philosophy is rooted in **Workflow Design**. Before writing a line of code or signing a vendor contract, you have to map out the actual human process. How does data flow from one person to another? 

I love drawing out workflow diagrams for real-world processes. Whether it's:
- How to schedule preventative maintenance on heavy machinery.
- How to determine the root cause of an engineering failure.
- How to submit an IT issue. (The first step on the diagram is "Talk to your supervisor," but people universally *loved* to ignore that one and just show up at my door).

When you build a workflow diagram, you make the invisible visible. This is especially helpful for multi-person teams who swap roles on rotations or between shifts. A defined workflow keeps everyone consistent. If everyone is doing the same thing the same way, you identify flaws and gaps exponentially faster than if everyone is doing it their own way. 

Only *after* the workflow is agreed upon do you select the software tools to facilitate that flow. 

## Choosing Your Battles

Part of understanding the human element is knowing when to fight for a system, and when to let it go. 

It isn’t always worth fighting every battle. When the owner of the company demands that you give him a blatantly insecure password, you do as you're told—but you make absolutely sure you document it in the exceptions log to cover your own ass. 

Ultimately, workflow design isn't about rigid enforcement. It's about building systems that make the right way to do things the *easiest* way to do things. When the tools match the human process, adoption happens naturally.
