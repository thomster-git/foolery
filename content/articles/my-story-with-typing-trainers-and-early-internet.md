I have been chronically online since roughly 2002. While other kids were learning valuable real-world skills, I was receiving an entirely different kind of education—one delivered through dial-up tones, glowing CRT monitors, and the wild west of the early internet.

My journey to basic computer literacy began in Grade 5, around age 10. Like any self-respecting pre-teen, I was spending my evenings on MSN Messenger, desperately trying to impress girls. Unfortunately, my efforts were thwarted by my own physical limitations. They bluntly told me that I typed so slowly it was actually hard to talk to me. It stung, especially since I was trying so hard to be cool. 

Seeing my digital social life crumbling, my mom downloaded a few keyboard trainer programs. They were simple: letters would pop up on the screen, and I had to press the right key before the time ran out. What started as a humiliating necessity quickly turned into a game. I got hooked on beating my own high scores, inadvertently teaching myself to touch-type at a blistering pace. 

It was a vast improvement from a separate, slightly earlier memory I have from Computer class. We were tasked with typing out a story we’d written for English. I was hunting and pecking my way through it when I suddenly needed to type a word with a 'Z'. I spent what felt like an eternity scanning the keyboard. I couldn't find the Z key anywhere, so eventually, I just... left the letter out of the story entirely.

As I got faster at typing, the internet started to open up. My early web diet consisted of eBaum's World, Newgrounds, endless hours of early YouTube, and the relentless grind of RuneScape. I lurked on some forums, though I was never personally very active in posting. 

Eventually, as you do when you spend too much unregulated time online, I found 4chan. From there, curiosity led me to figure out how to browse the dark web. I wasn't some teenage cyber-criminal, just a deeply curious kid poking around in the shadowy corners of the internet to see what was there. I learned how people exploited password recovery questions—using public information to guess maiden names and childhood streets to get into accounts—which immediately prompted me to tighten up my own security. 

Looking back, that era of being chronically online wasn't just entertainment. It was an education nobody was teaching in school. Learning how to pirate torrents safely, how to avoid limewire viruses, developing an awareness of the dark web, and understanding basic social engineering exploits—this self-taught digital literacy became the entire foundation for my career in IT. Long before I ever had a formal title for it, the early internet was training me for exactly what I do today.
