> **I get agitated when people in positions don't do their jobs correctly. Not because I expect perfection — because I expect honesty about why.**

---

## The Problem I Keep Watching

I've worked inside organizations where the gap between what was actually happening and what was being reported was enormous. Where managers were changing the rules, adjusting the guidelines, and redefining what counted as success — not to improve anything, but to make the numbers look acceptable on a slide deck.

The pattern is always the same. Upper management asks why KPIs are poor. Middle management, rather than explaining the structural reasons — insufficient team, wrong tools, no budget — rearranges the metrics so the numbers tell a more comfortable story. The underlying problem doesn't get addressed. It gets cosmetically resolved. And the cycle continues.

I've watched this play out in organizations with legitimate resource constraints, where the real answer is *"we don't have what we need to do this job properly"* — but that answer requires vulnerability and honesty that the organizational culture doesn't support. So instead you get performance theater.

That's what made me deeply frustrated. Not the broken KPIs themselves. The deliberate decision to hide them.

---

## What an Honest KPI Actually Does

A KPI that tells the truth — even when the truth is uncomfortable — does something cosmetically altered metrics can't:

**It identifies the actual problem.**

If a department's response times are poor because the team is understaffed, the honest KPI says that. If IT recovery time is slow because the backup infrastructure was underfunded for years, the honest KPI says that. If security incidents are rising because the MSP never escalated critical tickets, the honest KPI says that.

The number isn't the enemy. The number is information. And information is the only thing that can be acted on.

When you change the reporting method to hide the number, you lose the information. And then you're flying blind, making decisions based on a model of your organization that doesn't match reality.

## The Philosophy Behind Keel Systems

Keel Systems is a consulting practice I intend to build around a simple premise: **tell the client the truth, and help them act on it.**

Not the truth that makes the engagement sound larger than it needs to be. Not the truth that creates dependency. The truth that actually describes the state of their technology, their processes, and their organizational systems — and then a realistic, resource-aware path to improving it.

This came directly from what I experienced at CFI. We had an MSP that was effectively profiting from our organization's inability to audit what they were doing. They could bill hours on problems they caused and never escalated because no one on our side had the technical fluency to recognize what was happening.

The solution to that isn't just "get a better MSP." The solution is to build internal capability — or at minimum, internal literacy — so that the organization can be an informed participant in its own technology decisions. Not dependent. Informed.

That's what Keel Systems will do. Audit what's actually there. Report what's actually true. Build toward what actually works, with the resources that actually exist.

## The "Why" Before the "What"

I firmly believe people need to start getting down to the actual reason for things — the bottom "why." There is a lot of top-down thinking going around, and almost a shame for people who are bottom-up thinkers. If a piece of machinery keeps breaking and you just assume it's operator error, you're overlooking the possibility of it being anything else. Is the piece of equipment undersized for the job? Is there something else we may be overlooking?

The Mechanical Engineering habit that applies most directly here is the question I ask before I touch anything:

**Why is it this way?**

Infrastructure that looks chaotic usually has a history. Systems that appear broken usually made sense to someone at the time they were built. Before recommending changes, you have to understand the constraints — technical, financial, organizational — that produced the current state. That means not parachuting in with a pre-built solution and forcing it onto an environment that isn't ready for it. It means actually mapping the situation first. Understanding the load-bearing elements before you start pulling pieces out.

And then, critically: **reporting what you found honestly**, even when the answer is "this was never properly resourced, and the problems you're seeing are predictable consequences of that."

This philosophy also extends to how rules and processes are communicated. I highly appreciate when people can explain the purpose of *why* a rule is made in a particular way. For example: why do I need to provide security with a reason I can't go in to work today? Why is saying I won't be in not enough? Why does it need to be a call and not a text?

I had a similar realization at the mine regarding safety rules. I always thought it was so stupid and redundant that wheel chocks needed to go on light-duty vehicles in the parking lot. It made no sense to me — until the mine manager explained it was because the act of removing the chocks *forces* people to do a mandatory 360-degree walkaround of the vehicle before driving. Once the "why" was explained, I thought it was brilliant. 

### Structured Workflows for Universal Accessibility

Beyond just explaining the "why," I believe having structured workflows in place for tasks is extremely helpful — not only for people who are neurodivergent, but for neurotypical people as well.

Imagine simple flow charts that you can follow, equipped with decision and action shapes that guide you from the beginning to the end of a task. This helps standardize processes in the workplace so nobody can say they didn't know how to do something, nor stray from the corporate standard. They can literally put their finger on the paper and follow the line. That's the kind of clarity Keel Systems aims to build.

## Who Keel Systems Is For

Small and mid-sized organizations that are currently trusting external vendors to manage technology they don't understand. Organizations that have been told their old hardware is worthless, their network is fine, their security is covered — without any internal person capable of verifying those claims.

Organizations that are in the situation I walked into: inheriting undocumented infrastructure, dependent on an MSP relationship that isn't serving them, and without the budget or headcount to solve it the way a large enterprise would.

That's a solvable problem. It doesn't require enterprise resources. It requires honest assessment, clear prioritization, and someone who will tell you what's actually happening instead of what looks good in the quarterly review.

That's the job. That's what Keel Systems is built to do.
