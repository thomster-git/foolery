In almost every industrial and office operation I've worked in, I have watched the exact same scene play out at the end of the week: a smart, hardworking supervisor sitting at a spreadsheet trying to figure out how to categorize a delay so the weekly KPI dashboard stays green.

When good people feel pressured to massage a number instead of explaining what actually went wrong, the people aren't broken. The metric is broken.

## What Goodhart's Law Looks Like on a Shop Floor

When leadership treats a single target—whether it is ticket closure time, equipment availability, or daily output—as a punishment trigger rather than a diagnostic gauge, the number stops measuring reality within a month. Technicians close tickets early and reopen them under a new ID. Planners reclassify downtime buckets. The chart upstairs looks great right up until the day the system fails.

## Why I Started Consulting This Way

When I sit down with a small team now, I don't start by picking software or drawing a dashboard. I start by asking the people who actually do the work to walk me through what happens when a job goes sideways:
* Where does information get stuck waiting for a signature?
* Which spreadsheet is being typed in twice because two tools don't talk to each other?
* What number on the weekly report does everyone privately know is fiction?

Once you map how work really moves—and give people metrics that reward surfacing a bottleneck early rather than hiding it—the technology side (whether it is a SharePoint list, a Python script, or a Proxmox box) becomes straightforward.
