> **Unpacking early school transitions, peer dynamics, and learning unwritten social norms.**

My early school years spanned two distinct environments: kindergarten in the capital city of St. John's, followed by Grade 1 through elementary school in Carbonear, Newfoundland.

## Kindergarten Transitions & The Sandbox Breakdown

On the first day of kindergarten in St. John's, I walked directly to an indoor sandbox sensory bin. When told I was being assigned to a different classroom away from my friend Tristan, the sudden change in routine caused an overwhelming emotional breakdown. The teachers adjusted and allowed me to stay in that classroom—an early instance of environmental friction over routine disruptions.

Other moments stand out: coming down with chickenpox and having a classmate drop off homework while I stood in the kitchen in my underwear covered in spots; or my dad building a snow fort outside our apartment and jumping on top of it first to ensure it wouldn't collapse on me. Because my dad explained *why* he did it, I understood the logic immediately and appreciated the safety precaution.

## Moving to Carbonear & Grade 1 Social Friction

Moving to Carbonear in Grade 1 felt familiar because my grandparents lived there, but entering the local school brought new social hurdles. On the first day of class, overwhelmed by the crowd, I chose an empty table in the corner. When neighborhood kids came over, one girl's immediate reaction was: *"Oh my god, he's so fat."* 

As an undiagnosed autistic child with a high IQ, I struggled to understand why peer interactions felt unpredictable. In Grade 3, during a class election exercise, I handed out custom bookmarks ("Vote for Jonathan"), only to watch classmates tear them up in front of me. 

Later in Grade 6, during a substitute teacher day, I co-wrote a parody song called *"Frosty the Plowman"*. I don't remember much of it, just the line: *"Frosty the Plowman was a very crooked hole..."* followed by some light vulgar language (maybe "asshole" or "shit"). The teacher found it, chewed me out in front of the class, and reprimanded me for how inappropriate it was.

Right around the same time, I was taking a spelling test. It was the first time all year—and the first time in a very long time—that I actually got a spelling word wrong. Feeling overwhelmed by the mistake and wanting to get out of the situation, I needed an excuse to cry. I "accidentally" kicked my toe as hard as I could into a bookshelf. It caused my toe to bleed, which gave me the perfect excuse to let the waterworks out. 

My dad had to come to the school to pick me up, and we went straight to a walk-in clinic because the toe was already ingrown and infected. I was incredibly anxious about having the toenail removed. I hesitated so much in the doctor's office that the doctor got impatient, leaving to check on another patient just to give me "one chance" to prepare myself. When he finally did the procedure, he was very rough and it was super painful—but the relief was worth it. (For the record, one of the worst pains I’ve ever felt in my life was a separate incident where I accidentally hit my toe head-on into a kitchen table leg while sitting down to eat supper. It felt like a knife going straight through my foot.)

Each of these experiences built a catalogue of social observations that eventually formed my conscious masking system in adulthood.
