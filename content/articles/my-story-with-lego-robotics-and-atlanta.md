> **Winning provincials, competing at the FIRST LEGO League World Festival in Atlanta, and navigating literal communication.**

In Grade 8, our school robotics team achieved something we hadn't quite allowed ourselves to believe was possible: we won the Newfoundland & Labrador provincial FIRST LEGO League (FLL) competition. We weren't just a team of kids playing with toys; we were a highly coordinated unit, and our victory earned us a coveted spot to represent our entire province at the World Festival in Atlanta, Georgia. 

For me, this was more than just a trip; it was the ultimate validation of my obsession with how things worked.

## Engineering, Mindstorms, & Technical Precision

Building and programming the LEGO Mindstorms EV3/NXT robots appealed directly to my analytical, neurodivergent mindset. The rigid logic of the visual programming blocks, the tangible cause-and-effect of gear ratios, and the absolute predictability of a well-calibrated sensor—these were systems I could understand and master. 

I served as the secondary driver and the lead mechanical tuner. While others focused on high-level strategy, my role was deeply tactile. I ensured that sensor calibration was flawless, that our gear ratios provided the exact balance of torque and speed needed for specific field tasks, and that the chassis balance was optimized so our robot wouldn't veer off course during autonomous runs. 

It was an environment where my hyper-focus was not just accepted, but actively rewarded. I could spend hours adjusting a light sensor by a millimeter, and that millimeter could be the difference between a perfect run and a complete failure. 

## The Hotel Room Incident & Literal Communication

The World Festival was an entirely different scale of pressure. Thousands of teams, massive arenas, and the weight of representing our province. The evening before our first official runs on the international competition tables in Atlanta, our team gathered in the hotel suite. The anxiety in the room was palpable, vibrating at a frequency I could physically feel.

Sensing the immense pressure on the primary driver and myself, one of our adult coaches attempted to relieve our anxiety with what he thought was a comforting hyperbole. He looked at us and stated: *"I don’t even care if you get 0 points tomorrow."*

His intent was clear to a neurotypical brain: *Your worth is not defined by the score; just do your best.* 

But processing language entirely literally, my brain immediately flagged a factual inaccuracy. Our robot was designed to automatically score base points just by leaving the starting area, and certain penalties were impossible to accrue. I immediately responded, matter-of-factly: *"Well, we won't end up with 0 points because of the way the scoring rules work."*

Intended purely as a reassuring factual clarification—a way of saying, *don't worry, the absolute worst-case scenario isn't mathematically possible*—my comment was instantly misconstrued by the coach as argumentative, pedantic, and disrespectful. He threw his hands up and abruptly walked out of the room.

## The Shutdown

The shift in the room's energy was violent. Filled with immediate panic, profound confusion, and intense guilt over apparently offending an authority figure, I experienced a complete internal shutdown. 

This wasn't just being quiet. It was a physical inability to speak. The cognitive load of trying to understand *why* my factual statement caused anger, combined with the baseline stress of the competition, overloaded my nervous system. I sat completely silently, staring intensely at my cup, using every ounce of willpower to hold back tears.

When the other team members noticed the shift, they continued asking what was wrong, peppering me with questions that only deepened the shutdown. 

Then, a close friend—someone who had spent enough time around me to intuitively understand my processing style—intervened. He physically placed himself between me and the well-meaning questions, telling the room firmly: *"Guys, he's feeling like shit, leave him alone."*

Having my internal distress recognized and validated without needing to articulate it myself allowed the pressure valve to release. The coach, to his immense credit, returned shortly after to apologize. He had realized, upon reflection, that I meant no ill intent and was simply engaging with the literal words he had spoken.

## Reflections on Intent

This moment in Atlanta stood out for years as a clear, vivid example of how literal neurodivergent communication can be painfully misinterpreted. What is meant as helpful accuracy is often received as arrogant defiance. 

More importantly, it highlighted how powerful it is to have peers who understand your intent, even when you lack the words to explain yourself. That friend didn't need a diagnosis or a textbook to know what was happening; he just needed empathy. It's a lesson that has heavily influenced how I try to show up for others to this day.
