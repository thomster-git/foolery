As a kid, my first real exposure to video games was sitting on the floor watching my parents play the SNES. They’d play *Donkey Kong Country* and *The Legend of Zelda*, and I was mesmerized. But when it came to actually engaging with the games myself, I struggled. I had poor eyesight—and no glasses at the time—which made dark scenes incredibly hard to see. On top of that, my reaction times were slow, and I simply couldn’t figure out the navigation. 

Looking back, it wasn’t just the eyesight; my undiagnosed ADHD was already pulling the strings. The inability to maintain attention on linear objectives meant I often got lost or frustrated. When I got *Pokémon Yellow* for Easter, I had such a hard time beating the first gym that my mom actually had to take the Game Boy and get me past it.

My first real breakthrough in playing *with* someone was thanks to my dad. He told me about a game he’d played at work called *Syphon Filter 2*. We bought it and played it together constantly. We followed that up with *Perfect Dark* on the N64, spending hours running through missions together. Those are some of my favorite early gaming memories.

Then came the Xbox 360, and with it, a noticeable shift. My dad’s enthusiasm for playing games together started to wane. To this day, I’m not sure if it’s because I had suddenly gotten much better than him and he didn't enjoy losing, or if he was just over it. Either way, it was a bittersweet transition.

But the Xbox 360 era became my bread and butter. I dove headfirst into *Call of Duty: World at War*, *CoD 4*, *Modern Warfare 2*, and *Black Ops*. I also spent countless hours on *Guitar Hero*, *Rock Band*, the *Red Dead Redemption* campaign, and *Halo 3* (both campaigns and online). 

It was during this time that the social element of gaming became the real magic for me. The vibe of playing with friends, hopping into a lobby, and just talking for hours was exactly what my ADHD brain needed. The fast-paced, high-stimulation loop of multiplayer shooters locked in my hyperfocus, while the social interaction kept me engaged. I made a lot of long-term friendships through gaming online. My most recent gaming friendship started through *Overwatch* around 2018 or 2020: I joined a voice chat, we hopped into lobbies together, and we just gamed for hours. That’s exactly what my peak teenage years felt like.

Things are different now. Post-ADHD diagnosis, I understand my brain a lot better, but ironically, I’m finding it harder to get back into gaming. It’s harder to decompress and actually relax. It’s also much harder to summon the energy to hop on voice chat and socialize. Currently, I play a bit of *Overwatch* on the Switch 2, and some *MLB The Show 26*, but it’s not the same.

Instead, I find myself trapped in the guilt loop. When I have downtime, my brain screams that I should be productive instead of gaming. So, I end up paralyzed, doing neither, and just watching YouTube on autoplay for hours. 

Undiagnosed ADHD shaped my entire early relationship with video games. It dictated the hyperfocus loops that made me love multiplayer, and it explained my early inability to stick with linear single-player objectives. I’m still trying to find a balance, to recapture that feeling of just having fun without the guilt, but it’s a work in progress.
