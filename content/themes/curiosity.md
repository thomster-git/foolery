I've been online in a meaningful sense since 2002, which means I learned the internet the hard way — by breaking things, fixing them, breaking different things, and eventually developing a working model of how it all fit together through sheer accumulated contact with it. Learning to use torrents meant learning enough about networking to avoid the obvious traps. Avoiding viruses meant understanding what they were actually doing. LAN parties meant being around people who knew more than me and asking questions until I understood what they understood. None of it was formal education. All of it was curiosity pulling me forward faster than any curriculum would have.

I have a coin collection that I inherited and added to over the years, and every time I sit down with it I approach it the same way: work through what's there, figure out what it actually is, understand the history that produced it. It's not about the monetary value, it's about the investigation — the specific pleasure of starting with an unknown and ending with an answer. That same instinct is why the PS2 modding project is on my list even though it isn't urgent. I want to understand how the hardware works at a level below the official surface. The question is interesting enough that the project will happen eventually, the same way any project driven by genuine curiosity eventually does.

The knowledge graph at the center of this project exists because I kept noticing that the most interesting things happen at the edges — where one idea connects to something you didn't expect. A note about ransomware architecture that links back to something from mechanical engineering. A thread about autistic burnout that connects to a decision I made at work three years earlier. The graph isn't a filing system, it's an instrument for finding those connections before they dissolve. Curiosity, for me, isn't about consuming information — it's about following the thread until you find out what it was attached to.
