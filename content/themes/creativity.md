In Grade 12, I made YouTube commentary videos. They got mocked — not in a way that left a scar you could see, but in the kind of way that quietly teaches you that putting something of yourself online is a bet you're likely to lose. I stopped. Selfies went the same way, gradually. The version of me that wanted a public creative voice learned to route around itself, and by the time I was an adult there was a well-established habit of making things privately or not at all. FL Studio projects that no one heard. Sony Vegas edits that lived on a hard drive. The creative output didn't stop — it just went underground.

Laser engraving pulled some of it back into the physical world. There's something about the permanence of burning a design into slate or wood that's different from a digital file — it exists, you can hand it to someone, it doesn't need a platform or an audience. EtchCentric started as experimentation and became something that other people wanted, which was its own kind of recalibration. The feedback loop of craft is slower and less brutal than the internet's, and I've found that easier to tolerate while I figure out which parts of my creative voice are actually mine and which parts were just performing for approval.

Project Atlas is the most public creative act I've attempted since those Grade 12 videos, and I'm aware of that. It's a knowledge system and a writing project and, underneath both of those, an argument that my way of seeing things has value — that the connections I notice are worth documenting. I'm not sure I fully believe that yet, but I'm building it anyway, which might be how belief actually works. You don't start with confidence. You start with the thing you're making and see what grows around it.
