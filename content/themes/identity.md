I got my AuDHD diagnosis at 31 or 32, and the first thing I felt wasn't relief — it was the disorienting work of reinterpreting thirty years of lived experience through a completely different lens. Every social situation I had white-knuckled my way through, every rule I had consciously reverse-engineered and filed away for later use, every time I had looked at a room full of people and run a background process to figure out what the expected output was — none of that was character flaw or personal failing. It was an elaborate, exhausting algorithm I had built to survive in an environment that wasn't built for my nervous system. Knowing that now doesn't erase the cost, but it changes what I call it.

I identify as autigender. That word took me a while to find, but it fits: my autism shapes how I experience and express gender in ways that don't map cleanly onto most existing frameworks. I'm not stereotypically masculine — I cry, I get emotionally flooded, I care deeply and visibly about things other men are expected to keep at arm's length. I also have no desire to transition. Those two facts coexist without contradiction. What I've had to unlearn is the idea that masculinity is something you perform correctly, that there's a standard I was failing to meet. My gender expression has always been entangled with how my brain works, not with any particular category I was supposed to inhabit.

The masking was sophisticated enough that it worked too well for too long. High IQ gave me enough processing power to run the social emulation layer in parallel with everything else, which meant I went undiagnosed far past the point where the intervention would have been easiest. By the time I started unraveling what was actually happening, I was in burnout and had to reconstruct identity from something closer to rubble. What I'm building now — including this project — is an identity that doesn't depend on the mask. Less performative. More accurate.
