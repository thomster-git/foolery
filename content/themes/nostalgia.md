Friday evening meant going to the video store and renting a PS2 game you had approximately 48 hours with, which meant you played it with a kind of focused desperation that I'm not sure modern game ownership ever replicates. You didn't have the luxury of coming back to it. You figured it out or you didn't. LAN parties had the same energy — someone lugged a tower across town, someone else brought the ethernet cable, and you crowded into a basement with machines that ran hot and loud and felt genuinely powerful because you'd built them yourself, or watched someone build them and handed over the screwdriver at the right moment. Those were my first IT classes and I didn't know it yet.

I remember the Yu-Gi-Oh! card market before I understood what market dynamics were. Older kids with stacked decks that their parents had funded. The quiet unfairness of watching someone cheat and knowing the adults running it weren't going to do anything. I also remember wanting an Easy-Bake Oven and being steered away from it. Small things that stick around not because they were traumatic but because they were the first moments where I noticed the gap between how things were and how they were supposed to be. That noticing is something I never grew out of.

Billy Talent is a band that has been in my life long enough to carry actual weight. Standing in a crowd at one of their shows, the music loud enough that it's physical, and crying — not because anything was wrong, but because something was right in a way I couldn't have explained out loud. That's what nostalgia is for me at its best: not a wish to go back, but proof that something mattered enough to leave a mark. The past isn't something I'm trying to recover. It's evidence.
