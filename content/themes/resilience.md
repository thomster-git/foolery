The night the Akira ransomware hit, I was the only person in the building who understood what was happening. No incident response team, no senior to call — just me, the encrypted file extensions multiplying across the network, and a Datto backup that I had set up to be immutable because I'd read enough horror stories to know that the backup is always the next target. That backup held. I held. Not because I was unafraid, but because I had already thought through the failure mode before the failure happened. That's the shape resilience takes for me — not toughness in the moment, but preparation that quietly does its job when everything else is on fire.

My son was born with a collapsed lung. Shoulder dystocia. The room filled with people in about four seconds and then he was taken to the NICU, and I was standing there in a delivery room that had just emptied out, at the same time my employer was going through CFI receivership and my job was disappearing underneath me. There is a version of that story where I talk about how I kept it together. The honest version is that I held it together long enough for the moment to pass, and then I found somewhere private to fall apart. I've learned that's not weakness — that's load management. You stay structurally sound when it counts, and you let the deformation happen somewhere it won't hurt anyone.

For fifteen years I wore glasses for macular edema that no one ever connected to anything else. Then a sleep study, then a CPAP machine, then — gradually — the edema resolved. My father was told to lose weight and sent home. I was told the same thing implicitly, but I pushed until someone actually ran the test. Autistic Burnout is the same story told differently: years of something being wrong, years of compensating for it, and then the long, unglamorous work of actually recovering instead of just masking the damage. Resilience, for me, has never looked like bouncing back. It looks like finally figuring out what was breaking me in the first place.
