Most of what I know about IT I learned from gaming culture, which is an embarrassing sentence until you think about what gaming culture actually required in the early 2000s. To game online, you needed to understand networking. To use torrents safely, you needed to understand security. To run a LAN party, you needed to be able to diagnose hardware failures under social pressure, with limited tools, in someone's basement. None of this was framed as education. It was just the tax you paid for getting to do the thing you wanted to do. That's a learning structure that has always worked better for me than formal instruction: a real goal, real stakes, and the freedom to fail until you figure it out.

The Proxmox bent pin situation is a more recent version of the same story. Home lab work is learning at the hardware level — you order something, you install it wrong, you destroy something minor, you fix it, and now you understand exactly why the documentation says what it says. No amount of reading about ECC memory or PCIe lane allocation sticks the way a mistake does. EtchCentric material test grids are the same principle applied to craft: systematic failure mapped across a grid, every bad result a data point, the finished test card a record of everything I learned about how a material behaves before I trusted myself to engrave anything worth keeping.

Autistic Burnout recovery has been the most demanding learning project of my life, because the domain is my own nervous system and the feedback loops are slow and often ambiguous. What I've had to learn — and am still learning — is that the system I'm working with doesn't respond to the same signals I was trained to respect. Rest doesn't mean doing less; it means doing less of the things that drain a specific kind of resource I spent thirty years pretending I had in unlimited supply. That's not wisdom you read in a book. It's information you extract from a long series of getting it wrong and paying attention to what breaks.
