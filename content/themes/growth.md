When ADHD medication quieted the noise, I expected clarity. What I didn't expect was that the quiet would make everything underneath visible — the sensory sensitivities, the pattern-matching, the need for deep structure — traits that had been there the whole time but were buried under the static. That's the strange thing about growing through diagnosis rather than despite struggle: you don't discover something new about yourself, you finally see what was always there. The growth wasn't becoming someone different. It was the slow, sometimes uncomfortable process of becoming someone accurate.

My path doesn't follow a straight line and I've stopped expecting it to. I started in Mechanical Engineering, moved into IT work, and now I'm building things on the web. On paper that looks like drift. In practice it's the same underlying interest — understanding how systems behave — applied to progressively different domains. The engineering degree taught me to think from the foundation up, to account for failure before it happens. IT work taught me that human systems fail in the same ways physical ones do, just with worse documentation. Web development is where those threads are currently converging. Each transition felt like starting over and in retrospect was just a change in medium.

At my worst point during burnout, my music listening dropped from somewhere around 150,000 minutes a year to effectively zero. Music had always been one of the most reliable signals of my internal state — when it went quiet it wasn't a preference, it was a symptom. The slow return of music has been one of the more honest indicators that something is actually getting better. Not a dramatic comeback, just a playlist here, a full album there, occasionally something that lands hard enough to remind me what it felt like to be moved by sound. Growth, for me, often looks like the return of things I lost — not achievement, just restoration.
