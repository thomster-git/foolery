The first thing I did when I started EtchCentric was build a material test grid. Before engraving anything for a client or for sale, I ran systematic passes across different materials at different speeds and powers and documented what came back. It's the same thing I would have done in an engineering lab — characterize the material before you commit to a design. A lot of hobbyist laser work skips this step and you can tell: the settings are borrowed from a forum post, the results are inconsistent, the craft is borrowed rather than understood. I wanted to actually understand what the machine was doing to each substrate, not just find settings that mostly worked.

Building my second PC — GPU installed, aftermarket cooler seated properly, cable management done in a way I wasn't embarrassed by — felt like a different category of accomplishment from the first one. The first time you do something you're following instructions and hoping. The second time you're making decisions, and the decisions reflect taste. That distinction matters to me. Craftsmanship isn't really about the object you produce, it's about the relationship you have with the process — whether you're responding to what the work is telling you or just executing a procedure.

My RC airplane CAD model was the first project in my engineering program where I genuinely lost track of time in the good way. The aerodynamics were interesting enough that the work felt like investigation rather than assignment. I was proud of that model in a way I couldn't fake for the projects that bored me, and the quality difference was visible. Project Atlas is built the same way — not because I had to build it, but because the problem of organizing a life's worth of connected thinking into something navigable is genuinely interesting to me. Craftsmanship, in the end, might just be what happens when the interest is real.
